# Project Features

## Player Movement
- Walk, sprint, and crouch with smooth speed transitions
- Rigidbody-based so the player interacts with the physics world (can push and be pushed)
- Jump with coyote time (can jump briefly after walking off a ledge) and jump buffering (jump queues if pressed just before landing)
- Variable jump height — releasing jump early cuts the arc short
- Slope detection — player moves smoothly up and down slopes within a set angle limit
- Step climbing — automatically steps up small ledges without getting stuck
- Moving platform support — player inherits the platform's velocity
- External impulse support — explosions, knockback, jump pads can all push the player via `AddImpulse`
- Dodge (C, or optionally a double-tap of Crouch while moving) — several dodge styles, one active at a time:
  - **Sidestep + roll** (default, inspired by God of War): a quick sidestep; press again within a short window (or during the step) to follow up with a full roll in the direction you're holding. Skipping the roll only costs the shorter sidestep cooldown
  - **Committed roll**: one longer roll that can't be steered once started
  - **Steerable boost**: a long burst you can curve toward where you're steering, and keep shooting during; usable once in the air
  - **Long dash**: a fast, long dash straight in the direction you're holding (no input, no dash)
  - **Air dash**: a short dash that holds your height, usable once per jump
- Rolls and dashes have brief invulnerability (i-frames) and lock out attacks and abilities until they finish; sidesteps and boosts don't
- Dodging with no movement input steps backward (or forward, for dashes)
- A dodge pressed just before it's ready (end of cooldown, landing) still comes out
- Being stunned or starting a mantle cancels a dodge
- The HUD dodge indicator names the current move (STEP, ROLL, BOOST, DASH), turns green while committed and refills during the cooldown
- **During a dodge**: you can jump out of any ground dodge (the roll, sidestep, boost and long dash keep your height, so the jump carries on with the dodge's speed); the air dash holds you level, so there is nothing to jump from. While a dodge is committed (green) you can't shoot, cast, use items or throw; in its uncommitted moments you can. Melee and the offhand always wait for the dodge to end
- A stun or a mantle stops a dodge but not its cooldown, which keeps refilling
- You can't run and gun: aiming down sights (or raising a guard) or firing stops a sprint, and with Sprint held it stays stopped until you press Sprint again — so holding Sprint through a fight never breaks into a run (cancelling your reload) the moment you stop shooting. A toggled sprint is simply switched off
- Pressing Sprint while crouched (toggle crouch) stands you up and runs, once there's room to stand
- Optional stamina — sprinting can drain a stamina meter and stop when it runs out, and dodges can cost stamina; once emptied, stamina has to recover past a threshold before it can be used again (off unless a stamina cost is set)
- Slide — press crouch while sprinting to slide; launches at a configurable speed then decelerates smoothly; exits when speed drops below a threshold, the timer runs out, crouch is released, or the player leaves the ground
- Mantle — hold forward (W) and press Jump facing a ledge to pull up onto it, from the ground or mid-jump (a jump that rises to a ledge still catches it for a moment); only ledges between a minimum and maximum height can be mantled (up to 1.5 m above the ground you last stood on, so jumping first doesn't reach higher: a 1 m floor step or low cover can be climbed, a door-high wall, high cover or a railed ledge can't) — lower obstacles are simply jumped over, and there must be room to stand on top; the pull-up speed scales with ledge height (low ledges are quick, tall ones slower, tunable per height); works identically in first-person and third-person
- All movement values (speeds, jump height, gravity, etc.) are tunable in a ScriptableObject without touching code

## Camera
- Supports both first-person and third-person view
- Switch between views at any time with a keybind (default: V)
- Transition between views is smooth — camera slides between positions rather than snapping
- Aim point stays consistent during the transition — the center crosshair points at the same world direction in both modes
- Third-person camera avoids clipping into walls by pulling in when geometry is close
- Shoulder offset in third-person view, which fades out during the transition to first-person
- Shoulder swap — press X to flip the camera between the right and left shoulder in third-person
- In third person, shots leave from the character's eyes (lower while crouched) toward the point under the crosshair, not from the camera: cover in front of the character stops them even when the camera can see over it, and nothing between the camera and the character can be hit. When something blocks that line, a red dot marks where the shot will land (aim obstruction indicator). First person aims from the camera as before
- Shots land where the crosshair points at any distance, including past 100 m with a sniper in third person
- Player mesh automatically hides when close enough to first-person to avoid it blocking the view
- Sprint FOV kick — field of view widens slightly while sprinting
- Mouse and gamepad both supported with separate sensitivity settings
- Body rotation is snappy in first-person and smooth in third-person
- Aim Down Sights (hold right mouse / right stick) — works in both first and third person
  - Zoom (FOV) and transition speed are per-weapon: snipers scope deep and slowly, pistols barely zoom and snap in, ARs sit in the middle
  - ADS Zoom setting (Video → Camera): **Gradual** narrows the view along with the aim-in (Battlefield style); **Snap** keeps the view as is until the aim-in finishes, then snaps to the zoom (Modern Warfare style) and back out the moment you let go. Aim sensitivity follows the zoom; accuracy and aim-in time are the same either way
  - ADS Field of View setting (Video → Camera): **Independent** zooms every weapon to its own fixed FOV whatever your field of view is; **Affected** keeps each weapon's magnification relative to your field of view, so a wider FOV also aims wider (at the default 70° both are identical — e.g. a 45° sight becomes about 80° at a 110° FOV)
  - In third person: pulls the camera in closer and centers the shoulder offset
  - The held weapon smoothly raises from its hip position to a centred aim position and back
  - Reduces look sensitivity while aiming; all transitions are smooth
  - Movement slows to the ADS walk speed (a Player Movement Settings value) while aiming; aiming stops a sprint, and the cap never speeds up crouching

## HUD / UI
- Modular HUD system — elements (crosshair, stats) are independent and can be shown or hidden individually
- Key hints in the HUD show the keys you have bound, not fixed letters: the interact prompt, the dodge and offhand slots, the item slots and the weapon slots in the inventory. They update the moment you rebind a key or reset the bindings, and long names are shortened (LMB, RMB, Shift, Ctrl, Esc, Wheel Up/Down); an unbound action shows —
- Eleven HUD elements can have their built-in look replaced by a designer-made prefab: health, speed, ammo, dodge and artifact slot (a whole layout), and the ability bar, status effects, item slots, meters, notification feed and selection wheel (one slot, tile or row that the element repeats). The element drives the named parts it needs (fills, labels) and the prefab owns everything else about how they look; a skin that is missing a part is ignored and the built-in layout is used
- **Damage direction arrows**: when something hurts you, a red arrow appears around the crosshair pointing at it — ahead, behind, left or right of where you are looking — and turns as you turn, so you can find the shooter. One arrow per attacker (hits from the same enemy refresh its arrow instead of stacking), brighter for harder hits, fading after two seconds. Hazards, status damage and your own explosions show no arrow, and an attacker straight above or below has none either. Switch off with **Damage Direction Arrows** in Settings › Accessibility
- Every HUD colour comes from one theme asset (surfaces, text, status, accent), so restyling the HUD is editing that asset
- The HUD scales with the screen: it is laid out for 1920 × 1080 and grows or shrinks with the window, and the world-space prompt and damage numbers scale with it
- Crosshair drawn entirely in code — no texture needed; updates instantly when settings change
- The crosshair is the player's to customise on the Settings menu's **Crosshair** tab, with a live preview (half dark, half light so the outline can be judged against both): which arms show (all, no top, no bottom, horizontal only, vertical only, or none for a dot-only crosshair); the length and thickness of the horizontal and vertical arms and the gap from the centre on each axis (X and Y are set separately, so it can be wider than tall); an optional centre dot with its own width and height; opacity and red/green/blue colour; an outline with its own thickness; and how strongly it spreads with the weapon (0% keeps it fixed, 200% exaggerates it). Changes apply to the HUD as you make them, are saved with the other settings, and Reset Defaults restores the standard look
- Health bar that changes color from green to red as health drops, with a numeric readout
- Ammo counter displaying magazine and reserve rounds
- Stats HUD updates automatically whenever health or ammo changes — no polling needed
- Dodge cooldown indicator — a single slot to the right of the ability bar that drains and refills, matching the ability HUD style
- Floating damage numbers — every hit gets its own number at the point it landed, so a shotgun blast shows one per pellet. Hits soaked up by an enemy's shield show their number too. Numbers are white with a heavy black outline; critical hits are a deep orange, bold, drawn larger and held a moment longer, and bigger hits are drawn bigger than chip damage. Each number is thrown up out of the impact and arcs as it slows, popping in past full size before settling
- Restore numbers — an enemy or agent that gains health shows a green "+N" over it, and one that gains shield a blue "+N": heals, lifesteal, health regeneration, its shield recharging and shield siphoned from a Void Rift. Steady regeneration is added up and shown twice a second rather than as a stream of tiny numbers
- Damage grouping — hits landing close together form one group, scattered across a small patch around the impact rather than lined up, and the patch widens slightly as a burst continues. Each number is thrown at its own angle and speed, so no two take the same path and the group breaks up as it rises while staying on the target it came from. Numbers overlap freely where they cross — nothing pushes them apart. Numbers already in a group flinch when a new hit lands, so a sustained burst reads as damage piling up. Spacing and size hold the same on screen at any range, and the oldest numbers fade out early once the screen is carrying too many
- Hit flash and damage vignette — a red screen flash triggers on taking damage; a persistent red vignette around the screen edges grows stronger as health drops toward zero
- Velocity display — top-right HUD panel showing current movement speed in m/s, updated each frame

## Player Health
- Health with TakeDamage and Heal methods
- Armor reduces incoming damage (diminishing returns — 100 armor halves damage taken); enemies mitigate the same way
- **Armour plating**: a third pool between shield and health. Hits on plating are resisted with the plates' own armour on top of yours (+50 for the player), so a point of plating stands up to more than a point of health, and armour-piercing hits crack plates fastest. Plating never recovers by itself: only repairs restore it (Field Repair, regrown bone). Armour plating comes from gear and abilities: *Reinforced Plating* fits 40 extra plating for 12 s. The HUD shows it next to health
- Who lives on what: a character dies when the pool it lives on runs out. TECH machines have **no health at all**, only plating and shields, and die when their plating is gone; BIO and VOID die at 0 health (bone plating, where a BIO has it, sits on top). VOID lives behind its ward: about three quarters of its toughness is shield, the only pool that recovers by itself, over a little health. TECH machines don't bleed, but poison corrodes their plating like acid. BIO creatures carry bone plating over their health (+25 plating armour, about a fifth of their toughness); a bleed only starts once a hit gets past the bones, and then runs under them straight to health
- Shield absorbs damage before Health; any damage left over after a hit depletes the shield carries through to Health; shield regenerates automatically after a few seconds without taking damage; enemies can have shields too
- Hitboxes — characters can be split into head, body, and limb hitboxes; each region deals its own damage multiplier (defaults: head ×1 plus the weapon's headshot bonus, body ×1, limbs ×0.75), tunable per character type via a Hitbox Profile asset
- Explosions and melee swings damage a character once, no matter how many of its hitboxes they overlap
- Teams — the player and enemies never damage their own side, so your own grenades and projectiles can't hurt you; damage without a team (hazards, status ticks) hits everyone
- Fires a change event so the HUD reacts immediately without polling

## Status Effects
- Weapons, melee attacks, grenades and abilities can each list on-hit status effects, each with its own chance to apply per hit, and its own strength and duration: one Bleed (or Slow, Stun, Fire...) is shared by everything that causes it, a light cut bleeding less and for less long than a deep one
- A character has one of each effect at a time: a second source of the same effect (a snare's bleed on top of an ambush's) refreshes it at the newer source's strength rather than adding a second one
- **Slow** — each stack slows the target, up to 60% at most however many sources pile on; stacks wear off one at a time
- **Stun** / **Daze** — a stun stops the target outright; a daze slows it hard and eases off
- **Faction counters through damage**: TECH armour halves the physical hits BIO lives on, and TECH doesn't bleed (TECH beats BIO); VOID's true damage ignores armour and its cold makes armour brittle (VOID beats TECH); BIO's poison passes through VOID's ward to the little health under it (BIO beats VOID). Lightning still bites shields hardest
- **Bleed** — damage over time that ignores armor; reapplying refreshes it. It only takes hold when the hit that carries it reaches health: while a shield or armour plating soaks the whole hit, nothing bleeds. Its ticks then run past shield and plating straight to health
- **Poison** — stacks up to 5; each stack makes the ticks much stronger. It gets through a shield: it is applied even by a hit the shield soaks, and its ticks pass the shield untouched, into plating (it corrodes TECH hulls) and health
- **Fire** — damage over time based on the hit that applied it, still reduced by armor
- **Ice** — each stack slows and lowers armor; at 5 stacks the target is stunned; stacks wear off one at a time
- Stuns and slows from different sources combine sensibly: a new stun never cuts a longer one short, and the strongest slow applies — one slow wearing off doesn't cancel another
- **Lightning** — jumps from the target to nearby characters on the same side, weaker with each jump; more stacks mean more jumps
- **Shock** — jolts the target every so often for a little lightning damage (extra against shields) and a split-second stun that cuts off whatever it was doing; reapplying refreshes it
- **Cryo** — a chill (an Ice variant without the freeze): each stack slows and makes the target more brittle — armour counts for less against every hit it takes; stacks wear off one at a time
- A Slow never cancels a Cryo's (or Ice's) armour loss when they land on the same target or wear off
- Every faction agent and the player can be hit by all of them, and the player sees them on the status HUD
- Each effect chooses how repeat hits stack: refresh the timer, add stacks on one timer, or give every stack its own timer
- Characters can be made immune to specific effects
- Status HUD — a row of tiles above the ability bar shows the player's active effects with their stack count and remaining time
- All active effects are removed when a character dies

## Ability System
- Four ability slots (unbound by default; assign keys via the settings menu)
- Each ability has its own cooldown; activating it with no charge left does nothing
- Abilities can store several charges; spent charges recharge one after another
- Optional cast time: the ability fires after a short delay and is cancelled if the player is stunned, mantling or rolling; the slot fills up while casting
- An ability that wouldn't do anything isn't used (e.g. Heal at full health), so no charge is spent
- Abilities can cost a resource (mana, energy...) or Surge as well as using a charge; an ability you can't afford doesn't fire and keeps its charge, and its slot is dimmed until you can
- **Surge** (uncommon): most abilities only use a cooldown, but a few unique, potentially strong ones have their own Surge gauge (0–100%, a thin bar under the ability's slot). It fills from combat actions — landing hits (counted at most four times a second, so fast guns don't outpace slow ones), kills and parries — at the ability's own rate (e.g. 10% or 25% per action), and starts empty each life. When the fighting stops it drains after a short delay, again per ability: some drain all the way to 0%, some keep a reserve (e.g. 30%), some never drain and can be banked for the next elite
- Surge can be used three ways, mixed per ability: a **Surge cost** (e.g. 15% per use, with or without a cooldown — some have none at all and can be used for as long as you keep fighting well); **Surge scaling** (the cast spends everything from its cost up to its maximum and hits harder the more it spends, with extra effects past set amounts — fire early for a modest hit or unload a full gauge on an elite); and **Surge charges** (each time the gauge fills it becomes a charge, up to the ability's maximum)
- Six built-in abilities: Dash, Projectile, Heal, Shockwave, Targeted, and Timeline
  - **Dash** — a quick committed dash in the move direction (or camera forward if idle), performed like a dodge (same steering, animation and air limit); it uses the ability's cooldown, not the dodge's
  - **Projectile** — fires a projectile from the camera that deals damage (and optional status effects) on impact; configurable speed, lifetime and gravity drop
  - **Heal** — instantly restores a set amount of health
  - **Shockwave** — damages nearby enemies and launches nearby rigidbodies away from the player. Walls stop it: an enemy or object wholly behind a wall is untouched, while one still showing round the wall's edge, over a low wall, or pressed up against the wall is hit. Other enemies and loose objects in the way don't shield anyone
  - **Targeted** — damages and/or heals whoever its targeting rule picks: the enemy under the crosshair, everything in a cone, allies around the player, the nearest enemy, or an area where the player aims; it isn't used when it wouldn't affect anyone
  - **Timeline** — runs an ActionTimeline via TimelineAbilityRunner; only one timeline ability can play at a time; can be ground-targeted so its area effects land where the player aims
  - **Stat Buff** — applies a set of stat modifiers to the player for a while (included: Damage Boost, +30% damage for 8 seconds)
  - **Reflect** — puts up a reflect (see Reflects below)
- Included ready-made: **Cone Blast** (a targeted blast hitting enemies in front of you), **Ground Slam** (a timeline ability that smashes the area in front of you and knocks targets back) **Crushing Blow** (instant: 55 damage, 50% armour penetration, to the enemy under the crosshair up to 30 m — burst damage to stack with weapon hits), and four Surge abilities: **Arc Lash** (no cooldown; +10% per action, drains to 0%; 15% per use: 20 lightning damage to the enemy under the crosshair), **Overdrive** (25 s cooldown; +10% per action, never drains; needs a full gauge: +30% damage for 12 s), **Rupture** (6 s cooldown; +10% per action, drains to a 30% reserve; spends 20–100%: a crosshair strike from 25 damage up to 4× that, with bleed at 50%+) and **Static Rounds** (no cooldown; +25% per action, drains to 0%; each full gauge becomes a charge, up to 3, each 18 lightning damage to the enemy under the crosshair)
- All ability values (cooldown, force, damage, etc.) are tunable on the ScriptableObject asset

### Reflects
- A reflect catches incoming hits and turns their damage into something else. It can be put up by an ability or triggered by a melee parry
- What it catches: for how long, how many hits (one, or everything until it ends), from which directions (all around or only in front), and how much of each caught hit is stopped (all of it, or none like a vengeance that lets you take the hit)
- What the caught damage becomes — any combination of:
  - **back to sender**: dealt straight to the attacker, wherever they are
  - **a projectile**: fired where you aim (aim-based deflect), straight back at the attacker, or bounced off your facing like a mirror
  - **a beam or slash**: any action timeline, scaled to the caught damage
  - **healing** and/or a **temporary buff**
  - **status effects** and/or a **stun** on the attacker
- Reflects keep the hit's damage type (a reflected fire hit burns). They only catch hits with an attacker (not damage over time or hazards), work on the damage that gets past your guard (so a parried hit isn't caught twice), and reflected damage can never be reflected again
- Included abilities (all instant cast): **Vengeance** (the next hit within 30 s is taken in full, 75% of it goes back to the attacker), **Deflect** (for 0.6 s, hits from the front are negated and fired back as projectiles where you aim) and **Absorb** (for 1.5 s, hits are negated and half of them heals you)
- Melee weapons can give their parry a reflect: **swords** answer a parry with a **Riposte Slash**, a wide slash toward the attacker dealing 150% of the parried hit (set any category's *Parry Reflect* to give other weapons one)
- HUD shows four coloured slots at the bottom of the screen; a dark overlay drains away as the next charge recovers, and multi-charge abilities show their charge count

## Interaction System
- `IInteractable` interface — any world object can implement it to become interactable
- Player scans for nearby interactables each frame using a zero-allocation sphere overlap (configurable range, default 2.5 m) and picks the one being looked at most directly; objects can be given a higher priority so they win when several overlap
- Optional highlight — the object the player is aiming at can glow so it's obvious which one E will use
- Press E to trigger the interaction; prompts only appear when something is actually in range
- Hold interactions: doors and switches can require holding E for a set time; the prompt shows a progress bar and releasing early cancels
- Interactables behind walls or other solid objects are ignored (line-of-sight check, can be turned off)
- HUD prompt appears bottom-center of the screen showing a blue key badge (the Interact key, "E" by default) and the action label (e.g. "Pick Up  Assault Rifle"); disappears instantly when out of range
- Hovering a weapon pickup opens a top-right stat panel with the weapon's name, tier (colored — grey/green/blue/purple/gold for Common through Legendary), quality score, category, and a two-column readout of damage, fire rate, magazine, reload, range, fire mode, ammo type, recoil, spread and draw time; contextual footer adds headshot multiplier, pellet count (shotguns), burst pattern (burst mode), charge time (charge mode) and armor penetration when relevant
- Weapon pickups: place a `WeaponPickup` component on any world object, assign a `WeaponData` asset; picking it up fills the first empty loadout slot and equips it; if all four slots are full, your active weapon is swapped out and left behind in the pickup's place, keeping its remaining ammo
- Random weapon pickups: add `RandomWeaponPickup` alongside `WeaponPickup` and assign a `WeaponCategoryData` asset; each time the object spawns a unique weapon is generated with randomised stats drawn from the category's thresholds
- Ammo pickups: `AmmoPickup` adds a set amount of a specific `MunitionDefinition` to the player's shared inventory — every weapon that draws from that pool benefits at once
- Ammo caches: a resupply crate with a limited number of uses (3 by default). Each use tops up the reserve ammo of every weapon you carry to three full magazines; a use is only spent if it actually adds ammo ("Ammo already topped up" otherwise). The ammo inside disappears when it's empty. Cargo bays and docking bays always have one along a wall, lobbies sometimes
- **Restraint**: something can hold the player in place — a jaw trap, a tether. While held you can't move or dodge and can't interact with anything; a prompt below the crosshair names what is holding you and shows a bar that fills with every tap of Interact and drains when you stop. Fill it to break free, or it lets go by itself after a few seconds. Enemies caught the same way are stunned for that long instead. The dev console can also apply it (`/restrain`, `/free`). Shooting and abilities are not blocked and being held does no damage of its own — whatever holds you may
- Interactions use the Interact key one of three ways: a single press, a hold (a bar fills along the bottom of the prompt while you hold; releasing loses it), or a **mash**: tap Interact repeatedly. A mash fills the bar a share per tap and the prompt adds "— mash" and the key badge swells with each tap; stop tapping and the bar starts draining after about a third of a second, at a rate set on the object (0 never drains). Completing it fires the interaction, and a node with several uses starts again from empty. Any scripted or event interactable can be set to mash, with how many taps and how fast it drains
- Resource nodes (placeholder): hold Interact (or, if set to mash, tap it) to harvest a few times before it's spent. Which resource a node gives isn't decided yet, so the included node can be found and harvested but yields nothing. Parks have one or two
- Health pickups: `HealthPickup` restores a set amount of health
- Pickups only show a prompt when they would do something — no health pickup at full health, no ammo pickup without a weapon — so they aren't wasted
- Pickups (weapons, ammo, health, items) can be picked up even when hidden behind a wall or sunk into the floor, as long as they are within range and inside the camera view (anything off-screen is ignored); doors, switches and containers still need a clear line of sight
- Doors come in two kinds. **Handle doors** (the double swing door): press E on the door to open or close it, and it stays as you leave it. **Button doors** (every sliding door): press E on the button beside the frame — there's one on each side — and the door opens, stays open about 3 seconds, then closes by itself; press it again to close it early. No door ever closes on someone standing in its doorway
- Door types (`Prefabs/Environment/Doors/`): a double swing door (two leaves opening together, by hand), a door that slides sideways into the wall, a split door whose two halves part left and right, and a split door whose halves part up and down. Each comes in two flavours: a plain one that only you open, or a `Sensor` version that also opens by itself for enemies as they step up to it and shuts again about half a second after the last one leaves. Locks work on every type: a locked door, one waiting on a condition or one barred one-way never opens by itself, and its prompt shows what's holding it
- Doors are audible, by their design: pressing a button door's button starts its motor, which can be heard (4 m for a sliding door, 5 m for the vertical split, closing behind you half as loud), raising suspicion in guards beside it and drawing a look without revealing you where you stand; a handle door is nearly silent (1.5 m). How you move doesn't change a door's sound. Enemies opening doors are silent, so patrols don't set each other off
- Doors into stealth rooms open only for you: every ordinary connection to or from a Stealth room has a faction-styled door with no sensor, so a guard can't see through a gap you didn't open. Enemies can't open them, so a stealth room's guards stay in until the alarm
- Forcing a door: crouch at a button door and hold E for about 2.5 seconds to force it open by hand — a third of the way, silently, no motor. It stays like that while you're in the doorway and slides shut quietly once nobody is in it (or a few seconds after, if you never step through). Slower than the button, but nobody hears it. A locked, waiting or one-way door can't be forced
- Generated levels fit every ordinary connection with a sensor door that opens as an enemy approaches (you use its button), in the style of the faction holding that side of it: Tech rooms get doors splitting left and right, Bio rooms double swing doors, Void rooms doors splitting up and down, and rooms of no faction a single sliding door. Enemies pass through them freely, so rooms stay as open to cross as before, but the door still hides what is behind it until it opens. Locked, secret and one-way connections keep their own doors
- Locked doors — a door can require an item (e.g. a keycard); without it the prompt reads "Locked (Red Keycard)", with it the prompt reads "Unlock" and the door opens and stays unlocked; the key can be used up or kept
- Condition locks — a door can stay locked until several different things are done: "switch on both terminals", "raise the reactor and the cooling signals", "defeat the guard and pull the lever". The prompt shows progress ("Locked (Terminals 1/2)"), each step is announced ("Terminals 1/2"), and the door unlocks once enough have been done ("Terminals 2/2 — door unlocked"). Doing the same thing twice only counts once. Steps can be terminals, anything with an event (a lever, a boss's death) or quest signals
- Terminals: hold E for a moment to switch one on; its light turns from red to green and it counts toward its door
- Switches: press E on a `Switch` to toggle one or more linked doors remotely (switches bypass door locks)
- Generic interactables — terminals, levers, buttons, NPC conversation starters and similar can be set up entirely in the Inspector: a label, optional hold time, optional required item, single use or a cooldown, and events for what happens (and for trying without the required item)
- Item pickups — any inventory item (resources, consumables, munitions, keycards, rolled gear) can lie in the world as a pickup showing its name and count
- Chests and containers — open once to spill their loot; can be locked behind a key
- **Container types**, each with its own colour and what it holds:
  - **Weapon crate** (red) — one weapon (any firearm or melee weapon), sometimes with ammo
  - **Equipment locker** (blue) — armor, a shield, an artifact or an attachment, sometimes two
  - **Supply case** (green) — 2–3 handfuls of consumables: bandages, rations, stims, grenades
  - **Resource crate** (brown) — 2–3 handfuls of crafting resources: scrap metal, cloth, the odd data core
  - **Munitions crate** (olive) — 2–3 handfuls of ammo of any kind, arrows included
  - **Chest** (yellow) — anything and everything: 2–3 draws across weapons, equipment, consumables, resources and ammo
  - Weapons and equipment from any of them roll their tier the same way as every other drop (Legendary 1% → Common)
- **Containers where they belong** — about half the rooms of these places hold one container of their kind, against a wall at the far end from the way in: Armory (weapon crate or munitions crate), Security Office (weapon crate or equipment locker), Barracks (equipment locker or munitions crate), Medical Bay, Pharmacy and Ward (supply case), Storage, Cargo Bay, Workshop and Maintenance Hub (resource crate)
- **Container subtypes** — a container can turn out to be a narrower kind of itself, shown in its prompt before you open it ("Open weapon crate (Pistols)"). The same level seed always gives the same subtypes. A subtype only narrows what can come out; tiers and amounts stay the same
  - Weapon crate (60% have one): **Pistols**, **SMGs & ARs**, **Shotguns**, **Long guns** (sniper rifles and LMGs), **Bows & crossbows**, **Melee** — 10% each. Its bonus ammo matches: pistol crates hold light or heavy rounds, bow crates arrows, melee crates none
  - Supply case (50%): **Healing** (bandages and rations, 30%) or **Combat** (stims and grenades, 20%)
  - Equipment locker (45%): **Armor**, **Shields** or **Artifacts**, 15% each
  - Munitions crate (50%): one ammo type — **Light rounds**, **Standard rounds**, **Heavy rounds**, **Shotgun shells** or **Arrows & bolts**, 10% each

## Items

- Every item the player can hold shares one definition type, split into two families:
  - **Stackable** — resources, consumables, munitions and attachments. These have no per-item variation at all: a better version is a separate item at a higher tier, not a luckier roll. Wood is wood; ironwood is its own item
  - **Rolled** — weapons and armor. Every piece is unique, with its own quality score and stats
- Items are tagged with the reality they came from (Tech, Bio, Void, or neutral)
- **Quality and tiers** — rolled gear has a quality score from 1 to 100, banded into five tiers: Common (1–20), Uncommon (21–40), Rare (41–60), Epic (61–80), Legendary (81–100)
- **Tradeoffs** — quality decides how much total power a piece has; tradeoff axes decide how that power is spread. High damage is paid for with fire rate and recoil, a large magazine is paid for with reload time, tight spread is paid for with draw speed and sway
  - At low quality the tradeoff is brutal: a hard-hitting Common fires like a musket
  - At high quality the same tradeoff still shapes the weapon's character, but the cost side stays perfectly usable — a Legendary is strong across the board and still has a personality
  - A bad roll is possible at every tier, and a good Common can outperform a poorly rolled Rare
- **Attachments** — craftable modifiers fitted into a weapon or armor piece's slots. Their effects are fixed, not rolled, and many carry a drawback alongside their benefit
  - Fitting is fully reversible: attachments sit on top of the item's own roll rather than overwriting it, so removing one restores the original
  - Higher-tier gear comes with more attachment slots (1 at Common through 4 at Legendary)
- **Armor for every slot** — Combat Helm (armor, a little shield), Combat Vest (torso: the most armor, bonus health), Combat Gloves, Combat Greaves (legs), Combat Boots and a Utility Pack (backslot: health and shield, no armor) can all drop from equipment loot and roll their own stats by item quality. A full set of good pieces roughly triples your effective health
- **Included baseline items** — one of each kind to copy from: Combat Vest (torso armor rolling armor and bonus health), Extended Magazine (attachment: +25% magazine, 10% slower reload), Bandage (consumable), Scrap Metal (crafting resource)
- **Inventory** — holds counted stacks and unique items side by side, with no capacity limit
- **Starting contents** — the player can be set up to spawn with a list of stackable items (typically a munition stack per caliber they expect to use). Left empty, the player spawns with an empty pack and zero reserve ammo, so the first magazine is all they have until they find a pickup

## Procedural Weapon Generation
- Eight weapon categories: AR, SMG, Pistol, Sniper, LMG, Shotgun, Bow and Crossbow — each defined by a `WeaponCategoryData` ScriptableObject
- Every stat (damage, RPM, magazine size, spread, recoil, range, reload time, etc.) is defined as a min/max range with an optional bias value (0 = every value in the range equally likely; negative leans low, positive high)
- Bias < 0 skews the random result toward the minimum (e.g. SMG mags weighted toward 20–30 despite max being 50); bias > 0 skews toward the maximum
- Stat ranges reflect real-world and common game conventions per category:
  - **AR** — 600–850 RPM, 25–40 round mags (weighted 30–40), moderate recoil, 40–60 m effective range
  - **SMG** — 750–1100 RPM, 20–50 round mags (heavily weighted low, P90-style outlier), short range, erratic horizontal recoil
  - **Pistol** — semi-autos fire at 360–670 RPM, so tapping the trigger as fast as you can always gets a quick, steady stream of shots (no pistol fires slower than 360 RPM — hand cannons like the Desert Eagle and .44 Magnum sit just above it and hit hardest); machine pistols fire 730–1350 RPM; 7–20 round mags (weighted low), high per-shot kick, 15–25 m range; wide damage spread (20–55) representing everything from Glock to Desert Eagle
  - **Sniper** — from 40 RPM bolt-actions to 260 RPM marksman rifles (see weapon models), mostly 5–10 rounds, terrible hipfire, near-zero ADS spread
  - **LMG** — from 450–550 RPM heavy hitters to the 1200 RPM MG42, 20-round classics to 150-round belts (see weapon models), slow reload (4–8.5 s), wide hipfire, high sustained recoil cap
  - **Shotgun** — from 60–90 RPM pumps that kill in one blast up close to 170–300 RPM semi-autos and full-autos that need two, 2–15 shells (see weapon models), 6–12 pellets per shot, heavy per-shot recoil. Shotguns reach further than the usual run-and-gun shotgun: hip fire is for corners (a pump kills in one blast inside about 4 m), while aiming tightens the pellets enough to stay a threat at mid range — a typical pump still kills in one aimed blast out to about 12 m, two to about 20 m, and three or four at 25–30 m. Pellet damage falls off gradually from about 15 m to under a third by about 50 m, and the pellets spread wide enough by then that it is no match for a rifle
  - **Bow** — hold to draw, release to loose an arrow that flies and drops with real physics. A short draw shoots slower, drops faster and hits softer; tapping without drawing wastes nothing. One arrow is nocked at a time (keep holding attack to nock the next), barely any recoil, and nearly silent — enemies hear it from only ~8 m. From quick, light shortbows and horsebows to slow-drawing warbows that hit hardest, and compounds whose arrows fly fastest and flattest (see weapon models)
    - **Multishot**: some bows loose 2–3 arrows at once (single-arrow bows are the most common). A volley costs one arrow; each arrow deals less than a single shot, so landing several pays off
    - **Vertical / horizontal draw** (Weapon Mode key, B): every bow has two draws. **Vertical** draws slower but hits harder (+15%) with a much tighter aim, and stacks a volley's arrows in a vertical line. **Horizontal** draws about a third faster but hits softer (−20%) and looser, and fans a volley sideways. Switching drops the current draw; each bow remembers its own
  - **Crossbow** — point and shoot: bolts hit harder and pierce more armour than arrows and fly flatter, but spanning the next bolt takes from about 1.4 s (a pistol crossbow) to over 3 s (a hand ballista). Most are single-shot; repeaters hold 2–12 bolts, and about one in five is a tech crossbow with an **autoloader** — three-round burst or full-auto, fed from a 6–15 bolt magazine (see weapon models). Quiet (~10 m)
- **Modular Pistol** — the starting weapon: still a pistol, but the most versatile gun in the game, at lower damage than the specialists. Each one rolls two things independently on top of its stats:
  - **How it shoots**: semi-auto (most common), three-round **Burst**, full-auto **Machine** pistol (faster, lighter hits, bigger magazine), or **Scatter** — a pellet spread with short range, like a sawn-off shotgun (rarest)
  - **How it hits**: balanced (most common), **Heavy** (hits harder, fires slower), **Marksman** (DMR-style: hard hits, long range, tight cone, heavy kick, slow), **Rapid** (SMG-style: fast fire, lighter hits) or **Carbine** (rifle-style: a bit more damage and range, easier recoil, slower). However the two combine, it never fires slower than 360 RPM, so even a Marksman or Heavy pistol keeps up with fast tapping
  - The two show in its name, e.g. *Marksman Burst Modular Pistol*; a plain one is just *Modular Pistol*. It always comes with the **Tactical Knife** perk (see Gear Perks)
- Any category can be given firing styles and stat styles like these (weighted)
- Several stats (ADS bloom, recoil recovery fraction, recovery delay, ADS recoil multiplier, hipfire camera kick) are automatically derived from the category type and fire rate so the weapon feels correct without manual tuning
- Bows and crossbows both draw from the shared **Arrows** pool. Draw speed rolls with quality, so a better bow draws faster
- Create category assets via **Assets → Create → CGD → Weapon Category**, set the `Type` field, or duplicate an existing category of a similar type to start from its thresholds; values can be freely tweaked afterward
- Generated weapons now roll a quality score and tier, which decides where each stat lands inside its category range — an SMG still rolls SMG damage, just high or low within it
- Stats that carry a weapon's *power* (damage, fire rate, magazine, reload, recoil, spread, range, draw, sway) are driven by quality and the tradeoff axes. Stats that only give it *character* (recoil recovery, heat behaviour, burst timing) stay random, so high-tier weapons don't all start feeling the same
- Every generated weapon has a seed: the same category, tier and seed rebuild exactly the same weapon — name, fire mode, quality and every stat. A random weapon pickup can be given a fixed seed so it always offers the same gun
- Like a map, a weapon's seed splits into layers that can each be rerolled on their own: **quality** (where it sits within its tier — the tier itself never changes), **stats** (which way its power stats lean), **character** (its name, firing and stat style, fire mode and the stats that aren't driven by quality) and **perks**. Rerolling one layer leaves everything that comes from the others exactly as it was — reroll the perks and the gun shoots the same; reroll the character and its quality, stat leans and perks stay. A weapon remembers its rerolls, so it can still be rebuilt exactly. Melee weapons and shields work the same way. Weapons that were never rerolled are the same guns their seeds always gave

## Gear Perks
- On top of their stat rolls and attachment slots, generated weapons (guns and melee) and armor roll **perks**: fixed traits that are part of the item for its life and part of its seed (same definition, tier and seed → same perks)
- **How many**: by tier — Common 0, Uncommon 1, Rare 1, Epic 2, Legendary 2 — plus a 25% chance of one more. An item never rolls the same perk twice and only rolls perks that fit it (no reload perks on a sword, armor perks only on armor). Some perks only roll from a minimum tier up (Magnetized: Legendary only). Every weapon can parry with its quick melee, so parry perks (Riposte, Second Wind) roll on guns too. Weapons and armor each have their own perk pool
- Some gear always comes with a perk on top of what it rolls (the Modular Pistol's Tactical Knife)
- **Tactical Knife** (pistols and SMGs only — one-handed guns): quick melee becomes a fast offhand knife stab that hits much harder than a pistol whip, but holding the gun one-handed costs accuracy and recoil control (35% more spread, 30% more recoil). The knife parries like any bash
- **Passive perks** are always on:
  - On a **weapon** they change that weapon, like an attachment: magazine size, reload speed, multishot (a chance per shot to fire an extra one, or more), projectile velocity, crit chance and crit damage, status chance and status strength, armor penetration, range or melee reach, lifesteal, recoil, spread, draw speed, damage, fire rate / attack speed
  - On **armor** they change you while it's worn: move speed, ability cooldowns, health regeneration, lifesteal, status resistance, armor, health, shield, and weapon stats for every weapon (crit chance, reload speed)
- **Triggered perks** go off when something happens: a **kill**, a **hit**, a **critical hit**, a **parry**, a **dodge**, **casting an ability**, **taking damage**, **being healed**, **aiming down sights**, **starting a reload**, **finishing a reload** (the rounds going in), **using a consumable**, or **swapping weapons**
  - A weapon's triggered perks only answer while it's in hand, and its kill/hit/crit perks only for hits it dealt (grenades, abilities and an arrow still flying from a weapon you swapped away from don't count). "Swapping weapons" on a weapon means swapping *to* it
  - Armor's triggered perks answer to everything, whatever weapon you hold or whatever dealt the hit
  - Each can have a chance to go off, and a cooldown. A perk with nothing to do (a full magazine, full health) doesn't go off and doesn't use its cooldown. Cooldowns are per perk, so two items with the same perk can't be swapped between to trigger it twice
  - Perks can't set each other off: a heal from a kill perk doesn't fire "being healed" perks, so they can never loop
- **Buffs**: a weapon's buffs end when you swap away from it, so a perk never powers up the next weapon of a combo; armor's buffs run their full time. Triggering a buff again restarts its timer rather than stacking
- **Crits**: crit chance gives any hit a chance to count as a critical hit wherever it lands (a body shot hits like a headshot; a headshot doesn't double up). Crit damage raises the critical multiplier
- **Status**: status chance makes a weapon's on-hit effects likelier to apply; status strength makes them hit harder; status resistance makes them less likely to take hold on you
- **Included weapon perks** (placeholders to tune):
  - Triggered: Evasive Reload, Slip Feed (dodge reloads); Kill Feed, Quick Hands, Adrenaline, Reap (kills); Riposte, Second Wind (parries); Dancer (dodge, melee); Eagle Eye (aim → crit chance); Fresh Mag (reload done → damage); Precision Feed (crits refund a round); Arcane Loader (ability → full reload); Quick Switch (swapped to → fire rate); Leech (melee hits → stamina); Threat Response (hurt → faster reloads); Run and Gun (reload start → move speed); Stim Loader (consumable → half a magazine)
  - Passive: Extended Mag, Speed Loader, Split Shot (multishot), Hot Loads (velocity), Keen (crit chance), Deadeye (crit damage), Infectious (status chance), Virulent (status strength), Piercing, Long Barrel, Long Reach, Siphon (lifesteal), Stabilized, Choked, Quickdraw, Heavy Hitter, Rapid
  - Legendary only: **Magnetized** (any ranged weapon) — every shot strikes instantly in a straight line, with no travel time or drop, and punches through every enemy in its path, losing 25% damage per enemy passed; walls and other solid cover still stop it. Shotgun pellets and volley arrows each punch through on their own, and a bow or crossbow with it fires like a railgun
- **Included armor perks** (rolled by the Combat Vest):
  - Triggered: Reactive Plating (hurt → armor), Battle Focus (ability → damage), Evasion (dodge → speed), Adrenal Gland (healed → damage), Scavenger (kill → loads the held gun), Stim Rush (consumable → speed), Bloodthirst (kill → heal), Counterguard (parry → armor), Tactical Rhythm (reload done → stamina), Opportunist (crit → crit damage), Steady Breath (aim → less recoil), Quick Holster (swap → speed)
  - Passive: Fleet, Focused (cooldowns), Regenerator, Vampiric, Warded, Bulwark, Vital, Shield Emitter, Marksman, Deft Hands
- A short notification names each triggered perk as it goes off. Perks are listed under each worn item and weapon in the Character window, after the name for pack armor and pickups, and in shop listings
- Buffs and debuffs on the player reach every weapon stat above, plus move speed, ability cooldowns, health regeneration, shield capacity, shield regen rate (how fast it refills once recharging; the wait after a hit is unchanged) and lifesteal. Not yet wired to anything: sway and ammo reserve

## Weapon Loadout
- Four loadout slots on the player; press 1, 2, 3, or 4 to equip that slot. A slot is a **main** weapon plus an **offhand** (a shield, artifact or one-handed melee weapon): switching slots swaps both, so *sword + shield → shotgun → pistol + mirror* is one key press each
- **One item can fill several slots**: the same shield behind a sword in slot 1 and a pistol in slot 3, or the same pistol with a knife in one slot and a shield in another. Slots just point at carried items; an item leaves the pack when first slotted and returns when no slot uses it, and picking up a weapon never throws out one another slot still uses. Moving between two slots that share a main doesn't redraw it — only the offhand changes
- Set slots in the Character window (Tab): click a weapon, shield or artifact (in the pack or already in a slot), then click a slot's **Main** or **Offhand** line; click an offhand to empty it. A slot shows as e.g. *Glock 17 (+Tactical Knife)* in the HUD, the inventory panel and the weapon wheel; a slot with only an offhand uses fists
- The weapon wheel (hold a slot key) puts a spare weapon from the pack in that slot's main place, or swaps the whole slot with another
- The scroll wheel cycles through the filled slots (down = next, up = previous), skipping empty ones and wrapping around
- **Last Weapon** swaps back to the slot used before the current one, and pressing it again swaps back — a quick toggle between two loadouts. It follows the slot if the wheel swaps it elsewhere, and does nothing once that slot is empty. Unbound by default; set a key in Settings → Controls
- Switching to a slot with a different main equips it and cancels any reload in progress
- **Reload cancels**: a reload loads its rounds at a commit point (80% of the way through by default, per weapon) — the ammo counter refilling is the cue — and the rest is just the end of the animation. Anything that interrupts you cancels the reload — breaking into a sprint, dodging, swapping away, using an ability or consumable, readying a grenade, bashing or swinging a melee weapon, or losing control (stunned, mantling): before the commit point the reload is lost and has to be started again; after it the rounds are already in and the rest is skipped. Swapping away after the commit point and back within 1 s brings the weapon back without a draw, so a well-timed sprint, dodge or swap-back is slightly faster than letting the reload finish. Abilities and consumables follow the same rule, but the weapon still waits for them to finish, so they mostly save time rather than getting a shot off sooner
- **Weapon models**: a weapon category can list its real-world models, each rolled by weight with its own firing variants, stat lean and reload, so every gun of a name plays like that gun. A model's stats still roll across its whole range, but lean toward its style (an M14 usually hits hard and fires slowly; a G36C usually fires fast with little range). A variant leans toward its own style too: a semi-auto Glock 17 rolls like a Glock 17, not like the full-auto Glock 18 it comes from. Assault rifles roll 20 models in five series of four, each series equally likely: the **M-Series** (AR platform: precise, low recoil — M4, M16 mostly burst with a full-auto M16A3 variant, M14 mostly semi, M27), the **AK-Series** (hard-hitting with heavy kick — AK-47, AK-74u, AKM, AK-12), the **B-Series** (bullpups: rifle range with compact handling — FAMAS, AUG, TAR-21, F2000), the **H-Series** (battle rifles with 20-round magazines — FAL mostly semi with burst and auto variants, SCAR-H, G3, plus the HK416) and the **C-Series** (compacts: fast handling, short range — G36C, Galil, AM-17, QBZ-95). SMGs work the same way: the **MP-Series** (German MPs: controllable and precise — MP5, MP5K, MP7, MPL), the **V-Series** (vintage: slow, heavy-hitting, big magazines — Thompson, MP40, Uzi, PM12), the **X-Series** (machine pistols: extreme fire rate up close — MAC-10, Scorpion EVO, MP9, PP2000), the **T-Series** (modern tactical: versatile with the longest reach — UMP-45, MPX, APC9, PMX) and the **P-Series** (unconventional designs — P90 and QCW-05 with 50-round magazines, the near-recoilless Vector, the PP-19 Bizon's 64-round helical magazine). Pistols too: the **S-Series** (service pistols: reliable all-rounders — Makarov, M9, USP, MP-443), the **C-Series** (classics with small magazines — M1911, Luger P08, Walther PP, Mauser C96 with a rare full-auto M712 variant), the **A-Series** (automatics — Glock 18, with a less common semi-auto Glock 17 variant, TEC-9, the burst-firing Beretta 93R, CZ 75 Auto), the **H-Series** (high power — Desert Eagle, .44 Magnum, the Chiappa Rhino as the faster, softer-kicking revolver, and the Taurus Judge, a revolver that fires shotgun shells) and the **T-Series** (modern tactical — the near-silent, near-recoilless Ruger Mark IV, the armor-piercing Five-seveN, Tac-45, P250). Snipers too: the **B-Series** (big-bore .50s that kill with one body shot — Barrett M82A1, AS50, Tac-50, M200 Intervention), the **C-Series** (classic bolt-actions loaded a round at a time — Kar98k, Mosin-Nagant, Lee-Enfield, Springfield M1903), the **D-Series** (semi-auto marksman rifles on Standard Rounds — M110, M21 EBR, PSG1, QBU-88), the **S-Series** (Soviet and Russian — SVD Dragunov, SVU with a rare full-auto SVU-A, the suppressed VSS, SV-98) and the **T-Series** (modern bolt-actions — L96A1 with a harder-hitting .338 magnum AWP variant, M40 with an M2010 variant, DSR-1 with an SRS variant, JNG-90). Shotguns too: the **P-Series** (pump-actions — Remington 870, Mossberg 590A1 with a stockless Shockwave variant, SPAS-12 that sometimes rolls its semi-auto mode, the 15-shell UTS-15), the **A-Series** (semi-autos — Benelli M4, Beretta 1301, Mossberg 940 that loads two shells at a time, the soft-kicking magnum Beretta Xtrema 2), the **M-Series** (magazine-fed — the full-auto AA-12 with a rarer drum-fed variant, the full-auto USAS-12, Saiga-12, STF-12), the **H-Series** (high capacity — KSG, Tavor TS12, the drum-fed Striker, Neostead 2000) and the **C-Series** (classics — a Double Barrel with a Sawed-Off variant and the over-under Browning Superposed, which always hold exactly two shells and fire them as fast as you pull, the lever-action Model 1887 and the slam-fire Model 12). LMGs too: the **M-Series** (modern squad automatics — M249 SAW with a harder-hitting Mk 48 variant, Negev, the soft-kicking Ultimax 100, Stoner 63), the **S-Series** (Soviet and Russian — RPK, PKM with a steadier PKP Pecheneg variant, RPD, DP-27), the **G-Series** (general-purpose belt-feds — the 1200 RPM MG42, M60, FN MAG, HK21), the **C-Series** (classic squad automatics with small magazines — BAR, Lewis Gun, Bren, MG34) and the **H-Series** (heavy machine guns: slow to handle, huge belts — Maxim, Vickers, Browning M1919 and the .50 M2 Browning). Bows too, where a model also sets how long its draw takes and how fast (and so how flat) its arrows fly: the **L-Series** (longbows: slow draw, heaviest hits, arcing arrows — English Longbow, Warbow, the precise Yumi, Flatbow), the **R-Series** (recurves: balanced and accurate — the tight-aiming Olympic Recurve, Takedown Recurve, the quick Scythian Bow, the Turkish Flight Bow with the longest reach and lightest hits), the **H-Series** (horsebows: the quickest draws and short range, and much likelier to loose a volley — Mongol Bow and Magyar Bow, the fast-arrowed Gakgung, the heavy-hitting Manchu Bow), the **S-Series** (shortbows: light and quick — Shortbow, Hunting Bow, the smooth Pyramid Bow, the hard-hitting but loose Self Bow) and the **C-Series** (compounds: the fastest, flattest arrows and barely any kick — Hunting Compound, the tight-aiming Target Compound, the Speed Compound with the fastest arrow and hardest kick, the quick Short-Axle Compound). Crossbows too, where a model also sets how long it takes to span: the **L-Series** (light: quick to span, light bolts, short range — Pistol Crossbow, Hand Crossbow, the lever-spanned Goat's-Foot Crossbow, Sporting Crossbow), the **W-Series** (war crossbows: single-shot, the slowest to span and the hardest hits — Arbalest, Windlass Crossbow, the slightly quicker Cranequin Crossbow, and the Hand Ballista, the biggest hit and slowest crank of them all), the **R-Series** (repeaters — the Chu-ko-nu with a 10-bolt magazine of light bolts and a fast lever, the Double Crossbow that always holds two bolts and fires them back to back, the Repeating Crossbow, the Revolving Crossbow that loads its six-bolt cylinder one bolt at a time), the **X-Series** (tech crossbows — the XBow with a three-round burst and a rarer full-auto variant, the precise, long-reaching Pneumatic Crossbow that spans its bursts with compressed air, the light full-auto Bolt Caster and the heavy, big-magazine Bolt Thrower) and the **C-Series** (modern compound crossbows: fast, flat bolts and quick to span — Hunting Crossbow, the fastest Reverse-Draw Crossbow, the compact Tactical Crossbow, Recurve Crossbow). A model can use different ammo than its category: H-Series pistols take Heavy Rounds, the Judge takes Shotgun Shells and the M2 Browning takes Heavy Rounds
- **Reloading in steps**: each weapon loads either the whole magazine at once (most guns) or 1, 2, 3… rounds per step. A category can offer several reload styles by weight, each with the models that load that way, so the name always fits: tube-fed shotguns load shell by shell (the Mossberg 940 two at a time), magazine-fed ones (AA-12, USAS-12, Saiga-12, STF-12) swap the whole magazine, and break-actions reload both barrels at once; sniper rifles are mostly magazine-fed, with only the classic bolt-actions (Kar98k, Mosin-Nagant, Lee-Enfield, Springfield M1903) loading one round at a time; revolvers load one round at a time, though some come with a speedloader that fills the whole cylinder at once, and the Mauser C96 reloads from 10-round stripper clips. The reload style is part of a weapon's character, so it rerolls with it. Each step takes the reload time divided by the steps a full load needs, and the ammo counter ticks up with every step until the weapon is full or the reserve runs out. Every step loaded stays loaded, so any interrupt only loses the step in progress. Blast, start reloading, and as soon as a shell registers, bash: the bash cancels the rest of the reload and the shell stays in, ready for the next blast. A topped-up weapon only loads what it's missing. The pickup panel shows the time per step
- Each weapon keeps its own loaded magazine; reserve ammo is a shared pool per `AmmoType` — two SMGs share the same LightRounds supply, an SMG + sniper diversify across two pools, a hand cannon + sniper both compete for scarce HeavyRounds
- Inventory panel (press I) shows all four loadout slots, highlights the active weapon, and lists each weapon's name; empty slots are shown as "— Empty —"
- Item inventory panel (also press I, sits to the right of the loadout) shows a flat list of everything carried — stackable items aggregated by definition (so all Light Rounds show as one line with a total count rather than one line per internal 999-cap stack) plus each unique gear piece; a header shows slots-used and total weight against per-player caps that turn orange when exceeded
- Starting weapons are configurable in the Inspector via WeaponData ScriptableObject assets
- A slot can hold a melee weapon instead of a gun; it is equipped, swapped, picked up, bought, packed and dropped exactly like a firearm (melee weapons have no ammo, so ammo caches and revives skip them)

## Input System
- All actions are defined in one place and can be reconfigured without touching code
- Every action supports a primary and secondary keybind (e.g. LeftCtrl and C both crouch)
- Mouse buttons are also supported as primary or secondary bindings
- Each action can independently be set to one of three modes:
  - Pressed — fires once on the frame the key is pressed
  - Held — fires every frame the key is held down
  - Toggle — pressing the key flips it on or off
- Bindings can be remapped at runtime via the settings menu, driven entirely by the Input System's own interactive rebinding — no hand-rolled key/device translation code
- Every action can be bound to a keyboard key, mouse button, or gamepad button (any mix of two, primary and secondary) — rebinding listens for input from any connected device and picks up whatever is pressed first
- Default keys (keyboard plus left/right mouse and the scroll wheel — no extra mouse buttons, since not every mouse has them): Jump (Space), Sprint (Shift), Crouch (Ctrl), Dodge (C), Attack (left mouse), Aim (right mouse), Reload (R), Interact (E), Melee (V), Abilities 1–4 (Q, F, G, X), Weapon slots 1–4 (1–4), Next / Previous Weapon (scroll down / up), Last Weapon (unbound), Item slots 1–4 (5–8), Shoulder Swap (H), Weapon Mode (B — bow draw), Lock-on (T), Switch View (F5), Inventory (I), Character (Tab), Map (M), Pause (Esc), Console (`)
- Hold any weapon key (1–4) or item key (5–8) to open a selection wheel for that slot: move the mouse (or stick) toward a choice and release to pick it; release in the middle to cancel. The camera holds still while the wheel is open
- Move and Look are built the same way as every other action (WASD/arrows + gamepad left stick for Move, mouse delta + gamepad right stick for Look) — one input pipeline for the whole game instead of a separate non-remappable asset just for movement

## Settings Menu
- Built with the same runtime UGUI/TextMeshPro system as the rest of the HUD (not legacy IMGUI) — consistent styling and resolution scaling with everything else
- Press Escape at any time to open or close the settings menu
- Mouse and gamepad sensitivity sliders that apply as you drag them, like the other settings, and are saved when the menu closes; Reset Defaults on the Controls tab also resets them
- Full keybinding editor — every action shows its primary and secondary slot; click a slot then press any key, mouse, or gamepad button to rebind it
- While a slot is listening, tapping Escape cancels and leaves the binding as it was; holding Escape clears the slot so it is unbound
- Duplicate binding warning — the same key, mouse button or gamepad button can be bound to several actions; when that happens a brief orange notice names the other actions, and every slot sharing a control stays highlighted orange in the keybinding list until one of them is changed
- Reset to Defaults button restores all keybindings to their original values
- The menu has five tabs: Controls (sensitivity and keybindings), Audio, Video, Crosshair and Accessibility. Reset Defaults resets only the tab you're on
- **Audio** — Master, Effects, Music and Interface volume sliders, heard straight away
- **Video** — resolution, window mode (fullscreen, borderless, windowed), quality level, VSync, frame rate limit (when VSync is off), field of view (60–110°), ADS zoom (gradual or snap) and ADS field of view (independent or affected). Sprinting still widens the view on top of the chosen FOV. Resolution and window mode apply in builds, not in the editor's Game view
- **Difficulty** (Accessibility tab, under *Gameplay*; Normal by default): **Easy** gives enemies 25% less health and shield and cuts the damage you take by 30%; **Hard** gives enemies 35% more and raises the damage you take by 30%. The damage you take covers enemies, hazards and their grenades, not your own explosions. Enemy health applies to enemies that appear after you change it, so choose before a run. Run statistics don't record the level yet
- **Accessibility** — camera shake & kicks strength (0% turns off shake, weapon kicks and FOV punches), screen flash strength (hit flash and feedback flashes), the low-health red vignette on or off, and the damage direction arrows on or off. Hold vs. toggle is set per action on the Controls tab
- **Input Buffering** (Accessibility tab, off by default): a press made while you can't act on it yet is remembered for 0.4 s and happens as soon as you can, like queued inputs in MOBAs. Covers firing while a gun is drawing, reloading, between shots or during a dodge (so *swap, roll, fire* shoots the moment the roll ends), melee swings and bashes tapped during a dodge or stun (or a bash pressed while a swing or bash is still playing), abilities pressed while another ability is casting or going off, during a dodge or while stunned (so abilities 1→2 in quick succession both fire, even on the same frame), and consumables pressed while another is being used, on cooldown or during a dodge. A press is never carried over to a different weapon; raising the guard drops a queued swing
- Audio, video and accessibility changes preview live and are saved when the menu closes
- All settings are saved to disk and automatically restored on next launch, applied before the first scene appears

## Weapon System
- Data-driven weapon setup via ScriptableObject assets — create a new gun by filling in a single asset, no code needed
- Four fire modes: Semi-auto (one shot per press), Full-auto (hold to fire), Burst (fixed burst per press), Charge (hold to charge, release to fire; releasing below a per-weapon minimum charge cancels the shot with no ammo spent)
- Pluggable fire behavior per weapon — assign a `WeaponFireBehavior` ScriptableObject asset on the `WeaponData` to choose how shots are resolved; three built-in behaviors: **Hitscan** (instant single raycast), **Shotgun** (fires N independent pellets per shot, count driven by `PelletCount` on the weapon, each pellet either an instant raycast or a travelling round), and **Projectile** (lands close shots instantly, spawns a travelling round beyond that); adding new fire types requires only a new ScriptableObject subclass
- **Projectiles** travel physically through the world with per-weapon speed, lifetime and gravity drop (0 = perfectly straight — bullets, plasma; higher = arrow/mortar arc); they advance every frame and swept-raycast between their previous and next position, so fast rounds move smoothly and can never tunnel past thin colliders; they pass through their shooter and any trigger colliders and stop on the first solid hit, and give up at the weapon's maximum range
- Projectile physics (speed, lifetime, gravity, instant-hit window, charge multipliers) live on the equipped `WeaponData`, so one Projectile fire behavior asset serves every projectile-firing weapon in the game — each weapon carries its own bullet velocity, and an 800 m/s rifle round and a 60 m/s arrow can share one behavior with wildly different feel
- **Instant-hit window on projectile weapons** — close-range shots from a projectile weapon or a projectile-pellet shotgun land the moment the trigger breaks instead of waiting for a round to fly; the window is a flight time, so it scales with the weapon's own muzzle velocity and a fast rifle stays instant much further out than a slow bow. Beyond it the shot becomes a visible travelling round the player has to lead. Damage, range falloff and maximum range are identical either way, so there is no point downrange where a weapon's behaviour visibly changes hands
- **Charge scaling on projectiles** — per-weapon low-charge multipliers scale speed down and gravity up so a weak (short-hold) shot comes out slower and drops harder while a fully charged shot flies faster and straighter; a bow held only briefly lobs an arrow that plummets, while a fully drawn shot flies nearly flat
- **Projectile shotgun pellets** — a shotgun can be authored to throw physical pellets instead of instant raycasts: each pellet in the cone flies at its own muzzle velocity with optional gravity drop, so a distant target has to be led and the cloud visibly travels, while point-blank shells still register instantly through the same instant-hit window. Pellet count, spread, damage and falloff are unchanged either way
- Damage falloff — full damage up to an optimal range, then drops linearly to a configurable minimum at the falloff distance
- Bullets and shotgun pellets keep travelling past the falloff distance (1000 m by default) and still hit there, at the minimum damage
- Headshot multiplier — each weapon's headshot bonus applies when a shot or projectile lands on a critical hitbox (the head by default)
- Damage type and armor penetration per weapon (and per weapon category for generated weapons) — e.g. Lightning rounds hit shields harder
- Weapons can't fire or reload while stunned, mantling, or mid-roll
- Magazine is per weapon; reserve is a shared inventory pool keyed by `AmmoType` (LightRounds / StandardRounds / HeavyRounds / ShotgunShells / Arrows / EnergyCells). Reloading pulls rounds from that pool into the magazine; the HUD reserve display reads live from the inventory
- Weapons come loaded with one full magazine on first pickup; if you drop an empty weapon and pick it back up, it stays empty — the pickup grant only happens once per weapon instance
- Tactical reload (round in chamber) is faster than an empty reload, and every generated gun saves at least a set amount of time over a full one — better rolls save more
- Auto-reload: pulling the trigger on an empty magazine, or keeping it held as the magazine runs dry, starts a reload; with no reserve ammo left it plays the empty click instead
- Weapon stats are grouped into three user-facing families that map directly to how the weapon *feels* — Accuracy, Control, Handling

### Accuracy (where bullets land)
- Hip-fire cone opens wide; ADS tightens it dramatically (each cone is a per-weapon degree value)
- Aiming down sights is pinpoint accurate (0.01° cone) and removes bloom entirely on every weapon type except shotguns; shotguns keep some bloom while aimed, which grows as the weapon heats up
- Aiming a shotgun tightens its pellet cone to roughly a quarter of the hip-fire cone, so aiming is what makes a shotgun reach mid range
- **Bloom** — each shot adds to the spread cone up to a per-weapon cap; spread starts recovering a moment after each shot, so slow-firing weapons like shotguns and snipers tighten back up between rounds while sprays from automatic weapons still build up
- At maximum spread the crosshair still pops open with each shot and settles back, instead of sitting still at full size (accuracy itself stays at the maximum)

### Control (recoil and its buildup)
- Each shot kicks the camera upward (vertical) and slightly sideways (horizontal)
- Vertical kick has small per-shot jitter so patterns aren't perfectly predictable; horizontal drifts using a configurable left/right bias, giving each gun a personality
- **Recoil patterns per model** — every real-world model kicks like itself whatever its stats roll: which way it leans, whether it swings side to side or keeps pulling one way, how jumpy each shot is, how sideways it kicks and how far it drifts. An Uzi climbs up and to the right; an AK-47 climbs right then swings back across to the left; a SCAR-H, UMP-45 or QBZ-95 pulls up and to the left; a Vector, AUG, MP5 or PSG1 goes nearly straight up; a MAC-10 or TEC-9 jumps unpredictably side to side; a Glock 18 or Mauser M712 climbs hard and to the right. Semi-auto guns mostly snap straight up with a little sideways scatter, revolvers and the Desert Eagle flip up and to the right. A variant can kick differently from its model: the semi Glock 17 is far tamer than the full-auto Glock 18 it comes from
- **Horizontal drift mode** per weapon — *Alternating* (default) ping-pongs between the caps for a classic swaying spray, *OneWay* respects the bias direction the whole way to the cap for signature always-one-side pulls (AK-style hard right, e.g.)
- **Recoil buildup / heat** — sustained fire raises a per-weapon heat value that makes each shot kick harder and less predictably, up to a per-weapon maximum; heat cools off gradually once you stop firing, so short controlled bursts kick less than long sprays
- **Firearm Lab** (CGD › Weapons › Firearm Lab…, or *Open in Firearm Lab* at the top of any firearm category): the place to make and tune firearm models. It lists the category's models with the share of rolls each gets from its weight (editable in place), and *Add model* starts a new one from an archetype — blank, semi-auto pistol, machine pistol / SMG, assault rifle, marksman rifle, bolt-action rifle, pump shotgun or revolver — with *Duplicate* and *Remove* beside it, all undoable. The selected model is edited right below: stat scales, firing variants and their weights, ammo, reload steps, and recoil, where *Recoil feel* applies a one-click pattern (straight up, climbs right or left, side to side, wild, snap up, shoves back, heavy flip) to tweak from. The tables underneath roll the category the way the game does, at a tier and seed you choose, and show each model's average damage per shot, fire rate, damage per second, time to kill a 100-health enemy, magazine, reload, kick and hip spread, plus the min / average / max of every stat for the selected model and how often each variant and stat style comes up — so a change shows up in the guns it makes before it reaches a scene
- **Per-gun kick weighting** — each gun weights the directions of its shot kick separately: how far the muzzle rises, how much the gun swings sideways, rolls, hops, and — independently — how hard it shoves back into the shoulder (a gun can shove hard without climbing, or climb without any push). Some guns also roll the view with their sideways kick
- **Per-axis weapon sway** — look-lag and breathing drift are weighted separately up/down and left/right per gun, and sway-reducing perks and attachments (and sway-raising penalties) now change how much the gun sways
- **Moving and airborne spread** — a gun can open its cone while you run and while you're in the air, so moving shots are less accurate on guns that ask for it; the crosshair shows it
- Accumulated recoil is capped per burst so full-auto spray stays controllable — once the gun reaches its maximum climb it stops rising, but every shot still kicks the view up and it settles back before the next round; sideways drift either ping-pongs between its limits (Alternating) or pins at the bias-side cap (OneWay); caps reset the moment the trigger is released
- The held weapon model kicks too: from the hip it visibly rears up and rolls; while aiming it drives back into the shoulder with a small hop that always settles before the next round can fire, so the sights are centred on the crosshair every time a shot leaves — if the sights were on target, the shot goes there
- **How each gun moves when it kicks** — every gun splits its kick between driving straight back into the hand and flipping the muzzle up, like the real thing: a Glock, M9, CZ 75, Vector or the bottom-barrel Chiappa Rhino shoves straight back; an M1911, Tac-45, Desert Eagle or .44 Magnum flips up hard; AR-platform rifles and bullpups push back, AKs, battle rifles and classic bolt-actions rear up. Harder-kicking guns also shove back further
- ADS reduces recoil by a per-weapon multiplier and can hold a separate recovery fraction from hip fire
- Recovery is tunable per gun: 0 = BF-style (aim stays up, no return), 1 = CoD-style (full return to original aim); values between give a hybrid feel
- Player counterplay — if the player deliberately pulls down against active recoil, the recovery origin shifts to their new aim, so recovery never fights against intentional aim adjustments

### Handling (how the weapon moves in hand)
- **Draw time** — swapping to a slot has a per-weapon ready animation window during which the weapon can't fire or reload
- **Look sway** — the weapon lags behind as you turn and springs back into place; heavier weapons lag further and settle slower, lighter weapons snap back instantly. It follows how fast the view actually turns, so it feels the same at any frame rate and sensitivity, with a mouse or a stick
- **Idle sway** — a subtle Perlin-driven breathing motion is always present at hip fire, so the weapon never feels frozen
- **Move sway** — walking and sprinting cause the weapon to bob in a figure-8 pattern that scales with movement speed and stops as soon as the player is airborne or standing still
- **Steady when aiming** — all sway (look, idle, move) fades out while aiming down sights, so the sights always line up with where shots go
- All handling values are tunable per weapon: draw time, look-sway amount and recovery, idle amplitude and speed, move-sway amount

### Tuning
- All values tunable per weapon: RPM, damage, ranges, Accuracy (spreads, bloom, recovery, cap), Control (kick, jitter, drift, buildup, recovery), Handling (draw, sway), reload times
- Design targets for inter-category (AR vs SMG vs Sniper etc.) and intra-tier (Common vs Legendary) balance live in `WEAPON_BALANCE.md` in the project root — TTK targets per category, off-range falloff expectations, tier feel goals, and the derivation from `WeaponCategoryData` FloatRanges
- Hand-authored Tier 1 (Common) reference weapons live under `Data/Weapons/Ranged/T1/` — all 20 models each of the assault rifles, SMGs, pistols, sniper rifles, shotguns, LMGs, bows and crossbows (the H-Series pistols show the HeavyRounds and ShotgunShells overrides). These are the "what Common feels like" baseline for every category

## Melee Combat
- **The Melee key (V) is the other hand.** What it does depends on the offhand:
  - **An offhand weapon** (a one-handed dagger, sword or axe): tap V for its light combo, hold V for its heavy attack. Its **opening strike parries** like a bash; the rest of the string doesn't. Aiming stays on the right mouse, so a pistol keeps its sights
  - **A shield**: V is a **shield bash** that parries
  - **Nothing**: V bashes with the weapon in hand (below)
  - Holding something in the offhand costs the main gun some accuracy and recoil control: a dagger a little (+15% spread and recoil), a sword more (+25%), an axe most (+35%); shields from +10% (buckler) to +35% (tower shield). An offhand item replaces a gun's Tactical Knife
- **Quick melee** (Melee key, V, with an empty offhand) is a **bash** with whatever is in hand: a pistol whip with a pistol, a rifle butt with the other guns, a guard bash with a melee weapon, a punch with empty hands. A gun with the Tactical Knife perk stabs with an offhand knife instead
- **Parrying is done with the bash**, with every weapon: a hit from in front landing within the parry window from the start of a bash (0.2 s for gun bashes and fists, per weapon for melee weapons) is deflected completely — no damage, no on-hit effects. Parries set off parry perks
  - **Up close** (within ~4 m) a parry **staggers** the attacker briefly, and the weapon's parry reflect answers it (a sword's riposte)
  - **A parried shot** from further away is **deflected**: fired back where you aim, with the shot's damage
  - **Parrying too early is punished**: if the parry window closes with nothing parried, you're **exposed** for half a second — you can't guard or bash again, so whatever comes next lands. A bash is committed too: it can't be cancelled into another bash, so parrying is a timing gamble, not something to spam
- A bash doesn't stop the gun from firing, and kills with a gun's bash count for that gun's perks
- With a melee weapon: tap Attack for a light attack; hold it past a configurable threshold before releasing for a heavier finisher instead
- Light attacks chain into a combo string — pressing again while the current attack is swinging or recovering queues the next step, which fires the instant the current one finishes; the string resets back to the first step after a short period of no input
- Damage resolves through the same Effective Damage pipeline as guns and abilities (armor, penetration, damage type all apply)
- Hit registration runs continuously during the Active window (every physics tick), not a single-frame check — moving targets are caught mid-swing
- Three hit shapes per attack step:
  - **Thrust** — single SphereCast forward (stabs, pokes); resolves hitbox regions so headshots and limb hits apply their multipliers
  - **Sweep** — fan of SphereCasts across a configurable horizontal arc (slashes, backhands); each ray resolves hitbox regions independently; ray count and arc width are tunable per step
  - **Slam** — OverlapSphere at the impact point (overhead smashes, ground pounds); area damage with no region resolution, hits everything in the radius once
- Each target is damaged at most once per swing regardless of how many ticks or rays touch it
- Per-step critical multiplier — applied when a Thrust or Sweep hits a critical region (head by default); tunable per combo step so a heavy finisher can crit harder than a quick jab
- Debug drawing shows the cast rays (Thrust/Sweep) or overlap sphere (Slam) each physics tick during the Active window
- When a MeleeAttackStep has an ActionTimeline assigned, the Active phase is driven by the timeline system instead of the legacy hit resolver — the timeline controls what shapes fire on which frames, while Windup and Recovery remain time-based; a timeline's swing hit event makes the attack's hit volume travel along the swing instead of firing from a fixed spot, and deals the step's own rolled damage, crits and effects at the weapon's reach

### Melee Weapons
- Every **sword**'s light combo and heavy attack use swing hit timelines: slashing steps alternate direction (right-to-left, then back), thrusting steps (Rapier, the third strike of Cutlass, Katana and Zweihander) stab forward, and chopping steps (the old area smashes) bring the blade down overhead — the blade visibly crosses the space it hits instead of striking everything in an arc at once. Each sword keeps its own timing, reach and arc width
- **Daggers** and **Spears** thrust (a spear's third light strike is a wide sweep), and **Axes** and **Hammers** slash or bring only the head down overhead, so the shaft doesn't hit — all on swing hit timelines
- **Bashes** use swing hit timelines too: the guard bash, fists, pistol whip, rifle butt and tactical knife are short forward shoves, and shield bashes drift across the target as they drive forward. Only the fists' light and heavy punches still use the single-cast hit
- Melee weapons roll like guns (quality, tier, seed → the same weapon every time): **Daggers**, **Axes**, **Hammers** and **Spears** as broad types, and **Swords** as thirteen weapons of their own (below)
- Found like guns: as (rarer) loot drops, and on the Gunsmith's shelf (arming swords, axes and daggers)
- With a melee weapon in the active slot, **Attack** (left mouse) swings it: tap for the light combo, hold for the heavy attack. No ammo, reload or recoil
- Each type or weapon has its own combo and feel:
  - **Dagger** — very fast stab-slash-stab, short reach, a long parry window but a flimsy block
  - **Axe** — slower chop into an overhead slam, bites through armour
  - **Hammer** — slow area slams that crush armour; the sturdiest block, the tightest parry
  - **Spear** — long-reach thrusts and a wide sweep; a narrow guard
- **Swords** are thirteen weapons of their own, each with its own moveset, stats and grip rather than one shared sword combo. Curved and chopping blades can make targets **bleed**; two-handed swords leave no hand free for a shield or offhand weapon
  - **Arming Sword** — the baseline: two wide slashes into a thrust, a broad sweeping heavy
  - **Broadsword** — heavier and slower: two broad slashes into an overhead cleave that hits everything around the point of impact, the sturdiest one-handed block
  - **Rapier** — all thrusts: quick pokes that crit hard on the head, the longest reach, armour penetration and parry of the one-handers, and a long lunge for a heavy — but light hits and a weak block
  - **Sabre** — fast, wide slashes and a rising heavy cut; a great parry
  - **Scimitar** — a four-cut flurry of light slashes that can make targets bleed; a loose block
  - **Shamshir** — the fastest sword: a four-cut flurry that bleeds, and a spinning heavy that cuts all around you; the weakest block
  - **Cutlass** — short, hard chops into a thrust and an overhead chop heavy; well rounded
  - **Kukri** — very short and very fast chops that often bleed, cheap on stamina, a bleeding heavy chop
  - **Khopesh** — a hooked sickle-sword: heavy hooking cuts into an overhead hack that bites armour, and a hooking heavy that bites deeper and bleeds
  - **Cleaver** — a heavy butcher's blade: very short, hard chops into a hack, and an overhead cleave that splits armour and can make targets bleed; a poor parry
  - **Katana** (two-handed) — the quickest two-hander: wide cuts into a thrust and a lightning-fast draw-cut heavy that crits hard; a good parry
  - **Claymore** (two-handed) — huge sweeping arcs into an overhead slam that hits everything around it, and a ground-shaking slam heavy; a sturdy guard
  - **Zweihänder** (two-handed) — the longest reach and hardest hits: vast sweeps, a half-sword thrust that pierces armour, and a wide spinning heavy; the slowest swings
  - Two-handed swords are rarer drops than one-handed ones
- Rolled stats: damage, **attack speed** (how fast every swing winds up, strikes and recovers) and armour penetration follow quality; reach, stamina cost and guard strength vary per weapon
- Swings cost **stamina** (heavy attacks cost double), shown as a bar above the health panel; a swing that can't be paid doesn't start. Stamina refills after a short pause. (A character without a stamina meter swings for free)
- **Block** (Aim, right mouse, between swings or in a swing's cancel window): raising the guard slows you to aiming speed and stops most of the damage from hits in front of you; what it stops costs stamina, and running out **breaks the guard** (the hit lands in full). Hits from behind or the side, and damage over time, ignore the guard
- **Shields** are the blockers: with one held, the right mouse raises the **shield** instead of the weapon's guard. It lets far less through (about 3–30% depending on the shield and its roll), costs little stamina per blocked hit, covers a wide arc, and blocks gunfire as well as blows. A melee weapon's own guard is for parrying; it blocks worse
  - **With a gun**, raising the shield replaces aiming down sights (no zoom, hip-fire accuracy); the gun can still fire from behind it, and V bashes
  - **Buckler**: small, light on the gun, blocks least, longest parry window. **Riot Shield**: the widest arc and the strongest block. **Tower Shield**: blocks hits from in front **on its own** without being raised — so a gun can still aim — but every blocked hit costs stamina, it weighs most on the gun, and it stops covering you for a moment after a missed parry
  - Shields roll a tier like other gear: better ones let less through and bash harder. Found as loot (rarer than guns)
- **Guard bash**: Attack while the guard is up throws a block-breaking bash that parries (see Quick melee above); the Melee key does the same bash in one press, without raising the guard first. Bashing costs a light attack's stamina. Raising the guard on its own only blocks; it no longer parries
- **Parry** (bash timing, per weapon): daggers have the longest window, hammers the tightest
- With a melee weapon equipped the right mouse never zooms the camera
- **Cancel windows**: once a swing has struck, the later part of its recovery can be cut short by raising the guard, bashing or dodging. The hit has already landed, and the combo carries on: slash, guard (or bash-parry), then the next press continues with the second step, as long as it comes within the combo's reset time. Combo steps can be cancelled early in their recovery; finishers and heavy attacks only near the end, so committing to them still has a cost
- Dodging is never blocked by a swing. Dodging before a swing's cancel window wastes that swing (its strike doesn't happen if it hadn't yet) and restarts the combo
- **Combo weaving**: a dodge, a parry, an ability, a consumable, a thrown grenade, an offhand use (a ward, a channel, a tome's spell; one cut short by a stun doesn't count) or switching weapons between combo steps keeps the next step waiting for the weapon's **weave window** (2–3 s; daggers longest, hammers shortest) instead of the short idle reset, counted from when the dodge ends. Doing nothing still resets the combo quickly. Weaving only holds the combo open; the buildup comes from the combo steps themselves (later steps hit harder or carry their own effects)
- Abilities fired in a swing's cancel window cut its recovery short like a dodge; fired earlier, the swing simply carries on
- **Swap-dodge cancel**: swapping to a gun and then dodging skips the rest of its draw, so the gun is ready as soon as the dodge ends. Order matters: swapping while a dodge is already moving draws in full, so swap first, then roll. Melee weapons need no draw and come out instantly. Together with combo weaving this lets combos like *sword, sword → swap, roll → shotgun blast → swap → sword* land as one burst
- **Combos survive weapon switches**: each melee weapon remembers its own place in its combo. Switch to a gun, shoot, and switch back within the weave window to continue with the next step (switching away before a swing's cancel window drops that combo). Heavy attacks always restart the combo

## Action Timeline System
- A data-driven frame-data system for choreographing per-frame hitbox logic for any ability or attack
- An ActionTimeline ScriptableObject defines a sequence of events across integer frame indices (one frame = one FixedUpdate tick at 50 Hz)
- An ActionTimelineRunner ticks through the timeline, activating and deactivating events each frame
- Events are polymorphic ([SerializeReference]) and stateless — all runtime state lives on the runner's ActionContext
- Available event types:
  - **ShapeHitEvent** — physics queries (SphereCast, Arc, Sphere, Box) with configurable damage, dedup, and region resolution; covers melee thrusts, sweeps, slams, AOE circles, and cones
  - **SwingHitEvent** — a blade-shaped hit volume that swings through a list of keyed poses over its frames (slashes, chops, thrusts, wind-ups); sweeps through the space it crosses so fast swings can't skip a target, resolves hitbox regions, and hits each target once; angles, reach and thickness are set on the event, and it can follow the aim or an animated weapon's socket
  - **SpawnProjectileEvent** — spawns a projectile prefab via PrefabPool
  - **BeamEvent** — continuous raycast or SphereCast forward each tick (no dedup, intentional per-tick damage)
  - **SpawnZoneEvent** — leaves a lingering damaging patch of ground at a resolved position, tuned on the event like any other zone (size, life, tick damage, colour, status effects)
  - **ForceEvent** — applies a force impulse to the caster or hit targets
  - **SoundEvent** — plays a SoundBank at the action origin
- Each event can resolve its position in one of three coordinate spaces: CameraRelative (melee default), WorldOffset (ground-targeted), or WorldAbsolute
- Every lingering zone in the game — grenade fire and acid, Void's Rift, Bio's Snare, timeline zones — behaves the same way: it hits everything hostile in it once per tick, glows with the same slow pulse as Void's siphoning Rift, half see-through in its own colour, and fades over its last second, and characters see it as ground to step out of. A zone can also make a noise when someone first steps in, or pay some of its damage back to its caster as shield
- Events can use a shared dedup set (one hit per target across the entire timeline) or per-event dedup (each event tracks its own targets independently)

## Grenades
- Grenades are items: they're looted, bought, crafted and picked up like anything else, carried in the pack (Frag ×3, Smoke ×2 per stack) and put on an item slot (5–8)
- Pressing a grenade's item key puts it in your hand (the gun is put away); hold the attack button to aim. Each grenade type throws one of two ways:
  - **Charged**: the longer you hold, the harder you throw, up to the grenade's charge time, so a tap lobs it a few metres and a full charge sends it as far as it goes
  - **Fixed** (Call of Duty style): every throw goes the same distance however long you hold, so holding only lines up the arc
- A glowing arc is drawn from the throw point, accounting for gravity, your own movement and the throw's upward lift. It follows the grenade's bounces too — off walls and along the floor — until it comes to rest, where a ring shows the blast radius (an impact grenade's arc stops at the first thing it hits)
- Release to throw; the grenade has an arcade feel rather than a realistic one: it leaves your hand fast and falls with extra weight, so it reaches its target quickly on a flat, snappy arc. It bounces the same way off every surface — a low hop off the floor, a clean rebound off walls — losing most of its forward speed each time, then settles after a short roll, so it stops just past where the arc showed. It takes along part of your own movement. Its body blinks faster and faster as the fuse runs down. Your weapon comes back afterwards
- Aim, a weapon key, or the same item key again puts the grenade away without throwing
- Each grenade type sets its own throw mode (charged or fixed), throwing speed range, charge time, how heavy it falls, how much it bounces and how much speed it keeps on each hit, how quickly it stops on the ground, throw angle, fuse (a timer, or going off on impact), blast radius, damage and how much of it is left at the edge, whether walls shield what is behind them, how hard it shoves loose objects, its noise, camera shake, colour and optional flash
- Explosion deals damage in a radius, falling off with distance from the blast center, through the same Effective Damage pipeline as everything else. Walls and cover block it: anything wholly hidden from the blast takes nothing, but a target with any part showing (a head over low cover, a shoulder round a corner) or pressed up against the wall is still caught. Other characters, breakables and loose objects in the way never shield anyone. A fireball flash marks the blast and loose physics objects are thrown by it
- Included: Frag Grenade, Smoke Grenade (a smoke cloud that hides you and blocks sight lines), Contact Grenade (explodes the moment it hits something; faster and flatter to throw), Incendiary Grenade (shatters on impact, sets everything nearby on fire and leaves the ground burning for several seconds) and Stun Grenade (no damage; freezes everything in the blast in place for 3 seconds). They are found in supply cases like the others. Stunned and slowed firing-range dummies stop moving too

## Enemy AI
- Every enemy thinks with a behavior tree (see *Enemy Behavior Trees* below): it patrols (or guards), investigates, hunts, returns to its post, fights, regroups and raises alarms. A stun pauses whatever it was doing and it carries on afterwards; death is final until the enemy respawns (see *Enemy Behaviour States* below). The regular enemy types and tiers all use the ready-made melee tree
- Idle enemies are cheap: one with nothing on its mind makes a few decisions a second instead of one every frame (fewer still with nobody near), and is back to full speed the instant it notices anything, so it never reacts later
- Enemies find targets by team: any character on an opposing team can be detected, so no player reference needs wiring; teamless props such as breakable crates are ignored
- A character never hides itself: enemies can see, hear, shoot at and spot the bodies of other enemies and fallen allies, not just the player (their own bodies no longer count as cover between them and what they look at)
- A ranged enemy type can fire visible projectiles instead of instant shots; they fly at a set speed, so they can be seen coming and sidestepped. The shooter leads a target it sees moving, aiming where it will be when the shot arrives — change direction to make it miss
- Projectiles only pass through their shooter, never through other characters grouped with it
- Enemies can be spawned from the object pool and reused: a reused enemy comes back at full health and starts patrolling again
- **Enemy tiers**: only tier 1 is defined for now — the everyday roster (~100 effective health). Tier 2 and 3 enemies (and how they scale) come later in development; until then every room, whatever its tier, spawns its faction's tier 1 enemies
- Once higher tiers exist, how often they turn up depends on the room's tier (see Map Graph Generation): tier-1 rooms are almost all tier 1 enemies (10% tier 2), tier-2 rooms mix in tier 2s (45%) and a few tier 3s (10%), and tier-3 rooms are mostly tier 2 and 3 (45% each). Encounter waves (Holdout, Rift, Lockdown) follow the same odds
- **Random affixes** (for when higher tiers exist): tier 2 enemies roll one random affix and tier 3 two (tier 1 none), named in front of them ("Brutal Warded Void Tier 3"): **Armored** (+40 armour), **Warded** (+40 shield, even on factions that normally have none), **Vital** (+60% health), **Brutal** (+40% damage) and **Swift** (+30% speed). Each spawn rolls afresh
- Patrol follows an ordered list of waypoints, looping continuously; idles in place if no waypoints are assigned
- Investigate / Hunt: the enemy looks into what it noticed — see *Enemy Senses & Memory* — and heads back to its post once the search turns up nothing
- Two combat types per enemy: Melee or Ranged, selectable on the EnemyData asset
- **Melee** enemies close the gap and attack at melee range: stop, turn to face the target, wind up, then strike everything hostile in front; configurable wind-up and cooldown
- **Ranged** enemies maintain a preferred engagement distance — advance when too far, retreat when too close, and strafe sideways while at range; fire hitscan shots at the target when they have line of sight, with configurable spread; burst fire is supported (multiple shots per trigger pull with a configurable interval between them); shots resolve through the full damage pipeline (armor, shield, hitbox regions all apply)
- Stuns and slows (e.g. from Ice) stop or slow enemies the same way they affect the player
- State color indicator: mesh tints grey (patrol), yellow (alert), red (chase), blue (stunned), dark (dead) via MaterialPropertyBlock — no material instances created
- World-space health bar appears above the enemy on damage and fades out after a configurable delay; billboards toward the camera. A hit swells the bar slightly and leaves a white band where the lost health was, which holds a moment and then shrinks onto what is left, so you can see how big the hit was. Enemies with a shield get a thin blue strip above the bar that behaves the same way
- All parameters (health, speeds, sight, hearing, combat type, attack, ranged stats, alert duration) are tunable per enemy type via an EnemyData ScriptableObject
- **Tier-1 enemy per reality** (placeholder capsules), each holding about 100 effective health — the raw damage it takes to kill one — spread the way its reality defends (GDD): armour cuts every hit before shields and health, so armour multiplies how long the rest lasts
  - **Maintenance Bot** (TECH, Service tier): a white service chassis with a lit waist band and antenna. Armour first — 50 armour, 37 shield over 23 armour plating (+50 plating armour), and no health: it dies when its plating is gone. Slow but hard to crack; its shield comes back after 4 seconds
  - **Scavenger** (BIO, Prey tier): a low, hunched creature with a mossy back and glowing pods. Health first — 61 health under 16 bone plating (+25 plating armour) and 25 natural armour, no shield. Its bones break first; bleeds run under them straight to its health. Fast and quick to strike but light-hitting, with wide vision and keen hearing
  - **Husk** (VOID, Affected tier): a near-black hollow figure with frost at its feet and a shard hanging over it. Wards first — 75 shield over just 25 health, no armour; its ward recovers after 3 seconds. Break the ward and it is fragile, so keep the pressure on before it recharges (lightning bites wards hardest). Slow, short-sighted, hits hard
  - **Grazer** (BIO, Prey): a hornless, hide-coloured herd animal built to be eaten, not to fight. It wanders its ground at a stroll, stopping to look up, sweep its gaze round, stand still or graze, and goes to grass and water when any is about. It has wide sight (300°) and keen hearing, and is quick to bolt: it runs from a hostile it sees or from the spot of a noise, a shot or a glimpse, whichever, and keeps running until it has lost sight of the threat. Cornered with a threat on top of it, it now and then (about one time in three, no more than every few seconds) lashes out with a light strike. When a predator (Bio or Void) hurts it, part of the damage comes back as health over the next few seconds, so a grazer is hard to finish off. It drops meat and hide on death in high odds (about 85% each, in small stacks), whoever kills it
  - Each faction's rooms in a generated level are fought over by its enemy; Breach rooms mix two. Headshots land on a separate head hitbox, a coloured visor shows the AI state, and enemies show on the minimap and map once their area is explored

## Enemy Senses & Memory
- Enemies only know what they have actually seen, heard or felt. Where you really are is used only while they sense you; the rest of the time they act on what they believe, which can be wrong
- **Sight takes time**: a glimpse makes an enemy suspicious and its certainty builds while it keeps watching — fast up close, slow at the edge of its sight range, slower in the corner of its eye and when you're crouched or standing still, faster once it's already looking for something. Anything in view right in front of it is noticed at once
- Sight needs a clear line to your chest or head: low cover hides you only while crouched, and smoke and stealth still apply. Ducking out of view for an instant doesn't lose an enemy, but staying out of view does
- **Hearing**: footsteps carry by how you move — sprinting is loud, walking carries the enemy's hearing range, crouching is quiet and standing still is silent. Gunfire, swings and explosions are heard from their own range. Walls muffle sound to half its range, and a heard position is only roughly right
- **Touch**: bumping into an enemy gives you away, seen or not. Being hit tells an enemy roughly where the attacker is, not exactly
- **Concealment is a wall**: a bush (or smoke cloud) can't be seen through from outside it, and nothing standing in one can be seen from outside, however close — an enemy only sees into or through it from inside the same bush. Whoever is hidden in cover is only given away by attacking, shooting or casting, which shows them for a moment; footsteps can still be heard. A cloak (Bio's Ambush) is thinner: anyone within a couple of metres still sees through it
- **Under fire**: bullets that hit or whiz past within a couple of metres tell an enemy it's being shot at and roughly from where — even if it never saw the shooter. An enemy that is already suspicious also notices when you aim straight at it
- Hearing alone never makes an enemy certain — it comes to look, and has to see you to start fighting
- **Memory**: an enemy remembers where it last saw you, which way you were facing, and how you were moving: standing still, running, strafing or backing away. When you slip out of sight it guesses where you went. If you were running it expects you to keep going. If you were strafing or backing away it expects you only got a short way, probably behind something. If you stood still it expects you to still be there. It searches from that guess, leaning the way you were heading. Double back, change direction, or break sight while running and then stop, to fool it. Without anything new to go on it slowly calms down and goes back to its routine
- **Flanking around your view**: an enemy circling round to flank you avoids the side you were last seen facing and prefers to come from your side or behind. Turning to watch the way it is coming can push it to the other side
- **No shots into walls**: a ranged enemy checks the path of every shot (and its lead and spread) before firing, and holds fire rather than shooting scenery right in front of it or round a corner; if the target ducks out of line mid-burst the rest of the burst is dropped. Grenade lobs are checked along the whole arc and skipped if a wall, ceiling or corner would stop the grenade short of the target. Suppressing fire only goes out when it would reach the target's cover.
- **Watching like a person**: an enemy holding position over where it lost someone no longer stares at one spot. It looks along open sightlines toward where the target went, glances about every second or two within about 70° either way, and never faces a wall in front of its nose; if its spot has no view that way it steps to a nearby one that has. When it searches an area its glances at each stop likewise pick open directions
- **Faction balance (AI Simulator)**: Tech's bolts fly twice as fast (26 m/s) and hit harder, so they land on moving targets. Void's slow bolt is fatter and bursts on impact, hurting and slowing anyone beside where it lands, so a near miss still connects. Bio is a little slower. All three agents have the same 100 effective health, spent differently: Bio mostly health (80 health, 25 armour, no shield), Tech mostly armour (100 armour, 30 shield, 20 health), Void mostly shield (60 shield, 40 health, no armour). Bio strikes for 20 a hit. Void hits hard in melee (12), and its ranged attack is a single heavy bolt rather than a burst: 25 damage every 1.8 s, flying at 20 m/s and bursting 1.6 m wide on impact, slowing everything it hits by 15% a stack. It keeps a 13 m distance and its shield recharges quickly (2.5 s delay, 20 a second). It only withdraws to recharge once its health is below half, and at most every 15 s, so it spends the fight casting from range
- **BIO — Ambush** (every 25 s): its way out of a fight. When it has reason to, mostly being hurt but also taking a beating, being shot at or crowded, it slips out of sight for 7 s (rivals only spot it within a couple of metres), heals about 12 health a second, and the moment it vanishes bursts about 6 m off at a sprint — preferably somewhere no rival can see (behind scenery or into a bush), across or back against the way it was heading rather than where it would be expected to carry on, never towards its rivals, and not the same way every time — so where it was last seen is the wrong place to look. From there it breaks away to full cover, then prowls while it recovers: it circles its target 7–11 m off, a few metres at a time from one spot no rival can see to the next, pausing at each to watch, its eyes on the target throughout. So it comes back in knowing where its target is, and from a side it wasn't last seen on. Rivals that lose sight of a cloaked agent lose track of it straight away, instead of following it for a moment as they would a target slipping behind a pillar. Its next strike, out of hiding, hits for two and a half times the damage, makes whatever it hits bleed for 5 s (which BIO can then follow by scent), and ends the cloak. It does not let that strike go to waste: once it has had about 3 s to heal, it stops recovering, whatever it was doing, and goes straight for its target to land the empowered strike while it is still hidden (giving up after about 4 s). While cloaked its body turns see-through (about a quarter visible), on top of the green glow, so you can tell it is hidden
- **TECH — Shield Discharge** (every 22 s): with someone within 5 m and its shield up, and a reason to (the target right on top of it, being hit, shot at, or several in the blast; otherwise after a few seconds), it spends the whole shield in a burst around it: lightning damage in proportion to the shield given up (30 shield ≈ 30 damage) to everything within 6 m that a wall doesn't wholly hide (a target round a corner's edge, over low cover or pressed against the wall is still caught), shocking them for 3 s (a jolt every 0.75 s: 15% of the burst's damage and a quarter-second stun), slowing them by 30% a stack for 4 seconds and knocking them back about 3 m (unable to act until they land) — a disengage. It leaves it with no shield but 40% faster for 3 s, so it gets clear to long-range cover straight afterwards
- **Void coven (AI Simulator)**: Void agents now fight as a squad like Tech and Bio, sharing what they see over the radio and focusing fire, instead of acting alone. Reserve Voids brought in with Squad sizes join it. For now it has no roles, so it is teamwork in awareness only
- **VOID — hybrid pacing**: its bolt comes every 1.8 seconds, its Void Rift every 14 seconds
- **Reactions**: an enemy that notices something first stops and stares, then goes to look, then scans and checks a few spots nearby — most of them the way you seemed to be heading. Something new on the way (another noise, a glimpse) makes it glance over and change course. Pauses, routes and the number of spots vary every time
- **Curious vs hunting**: an enemy that only noticed something wanders over at patrol speed and checks one spot; one that knows someone is about (it lost you, was hit, or heard an alarm) hurries, searches more spots and keeps at it longer
- **Hunting thinks about where you could have got to**: once it has lost you, a hunting enemy works out where you might be by now — starting where it last knew you were, leaning the way you were going, and spreading out along the ways you could have walked as time passes (never through a wall). Everywhere it looks and finds empty on the way is crossed off, and it heads for the likeliest place it hasn't looked yet, hiding places behind cover first. So the longer you stay hidden the wider it searches, it doesn't recheck a corner it has just seen, and it gives up once everywhere likely has been seen rather than at a fixed count. Crouching behind low cover still hides you from a glance across the room. A squad hunts together: what one member sees is crossed off for all of them, and each heads for a different place, so they fan out instead of bunching up
- **Allies**: an enemy that spots you calls out, and allies who could hear the shout (half as far through walls) come to where it saw you. Allies' gunfire draws nearby enemies in too
- **Temperaments**: Tech bots are wary — slow to notice, but they remember long and search thoroughly. Bio creatures are skittish — quick to notice and quick to give up, darting about. Void enemies sit in between
- **Read-out**: a mark over each enemy shows what it believes — a yellow "?" that grows as it makes up its mind, an orange "?" while it hunts, and a red "!" the moment it has you
- Cheap to run: each enemy looks and listens a few times a second, staggered so they don't all check on the same frame, with a handful of raycasts per check

## Enemy Behaviour States
- **Guarding**: an enemy without a patrol route stands at its post and looks around now and then, so there's no fixed blind side to sneak past
- **Investigate vs Hunt**: an enemy that merely noticed something investigates (patrol speed, one spot); one that knows someone is about hunts (faster, more spots, longer, checking cover you could hide behind). An investigation turns into a hunt if it learns more on the way
- **Staying in the fight**: when an enemy that was fighting you loses sight of you, it stays in combat for about 30 seconds instead of calming down within ten or so. For the first half it keeps fighting on its own terms (flanking, closing in, taking up an overwatch spot, checking where you were likely to go); for the rest it hunts, reasoning about where you could have got to and checking those places. If you show yourself again it re-engages at once. If it finds nothing by then, or runs out of places to check, it gives up, stays a little on edge, and goes back to its post
- **Looking around while on the move**: an enemy walking to a spot, flanking, falling back, hunting or heading home no longer stares down its path or at its target. Every few seconds it glances to a flank, behind it (when wary) or toward where it last felt a threat, sometimes sweeping both sides — so it can spot enemies, notice bodies and learn the ground as it goes. The more on edge it is, the more often it looks (calm every 4–8 s, wary 2–4 s, in a fight 1.5–3 s). It never glances while aiming, shooting, attacking, hopping cover or dodging
- Enemies never take cover against the outer walls of a room or arena, only against things standing in the space (crates, pillars, partition stubs)
- **On edge**: after a search that found nothing, an enemy stays on edge for about 45 seconds — it notices you faster, walks its patrol quicker and, guarding, looks around twice as often. The same trick rarely works twice in a row
- **Return to post**: after giving up ("must've been nothing" — a moment standing there looking round) or after killing you (a moment over the body), it walks back to its post or the nearest point of its route before patrolling again
- **Taking turns in melee**: only a couple of enemies (per enemy type) fight one target up close at once; the rest circle at a distance, waiting their turn, and an enemy steps back after a couple of strikes to let another in. Likewise only a few fire bursts at one target at once
- **Responsive movement**: enemies get up to speed, turn corners and stop within a fraction of a second however fast they are going, and come out of a dodge or dash at their normal pace instead of skidding on — tight, deliberate movement rather than sliding (tunable per enemy type)
- **Knockback**: some attacks throw a character back a few metres; they can't walk or act until they land, and walls and ledges stop the push
- **Stagger**: a single heavy hit (a quarter of its health by default) knocks an enemy off balance for a moment, spoiling an attack it was winding up. It can't be staggered again for a couple of seconds
- **Out of reach**: if you're somewhere it can't walk to, a melee enemy holds at the closest point facing you instead of pressing into a wall
- **Home territory**: enemies placed in a generated room range over their faction's whole territory — every connected room the same faction holds — but not beyond it. Chasing you, they follow across the territory and out through its border doorway, then a little way down the corridor (about 2.5 m past the outermost walls by default — just far enough to stand in the doorway), and hold at that edge — a standoff at the border — instead of following you into another faction's rooms. Out of sight, they search as usual and give up, then walk home. Each faction can set how far past its border it follows, or follow anywhere. Shots, shouts, radio calls and alarms raised outside a territory (past that same distance) are ignored by its enemies, so setting off an alarm in one territory never draws in the next — sneaking through a new zone starts fresh. A room with no faction is a territory of its own. A rival faction's enemy that steps into a territory breaks the peace: rivals normally ignore each other until a fight starts, but a calm enemy turns on a rival the moment it enters its territory (rooms a Breach room shares between two factions don't count). It still only picks the fight if its faction is willing to fight that rival; factions that avoid or flee it stay out of it. Enemies an encounter brings into a room (waves, boss adds) are held to that room, on top of the shutters that seal it; the director's reinforcements and hand-placed enemies are not held
- **Morale**: seeing an ally go down and getting badly hurt shake an enemy. Shaken, it falls back to cover or its post and holds there until it has steadied, then rejoins the fight. If it loses track of the threat altogether while holding, it stops holding and goes back to its routine rather than standing there until its nerve returns. Cornered, it fights. Skittish Bio creatures break sooner than wary Tech bots, and a Bio creature falls back into a bush well away from the threat (at least 9 m from it, within about 25 m of a run) to lie low in, rather than running all the way home; with no bush in reach it falls back to cover
- **Bodies**: enemies react to allies' bodies only when they actually see one (or hear the death nearby): morale drops and they start hunting from the body. Fighting out of sight keeps the others calm
- **Alarms**: some enemies run for an alarm panel near their post the moment they spot you. Raised, every ally within about 60 m hears it (walls don't stop it) and comes looking where you were last seen. Stop the runner or disable the panel first (hold Interact); it re-arms 30 seconds after sounding
- The enemy's colour shows its state: grey patrolling, yellow investigating or heading home, orange hunting, red fighting, purple regrouping, blue stunned

## Enemy Tactics: Cover & Flanking
- Levels are scanned for cover when they load: every spot along a wall, crate or pillar an enemy could stand behind, marked low (waist-high — it can shoot over) or high (hides it completely)
- An enemy only uses cover it knows: the area around where it spawned and along its patrol route, plus spots it has seen since. In unfamiliar rooms it has to look around first
- **Cover against you**: an enemy picks cover whose obstacle stands between it and where it *believes* you are, near the range it likes to fight at, and checks the line from you before committing, taking the best of its few favourite spots that passes. Two enemies don't crowd behind the same spot
- **Ranged enemies** run to cover and shoot from it while it still screens them, and move to new cover every 6–10 seconds or as soon as you've worked round it. With no cover around they keep their distance and strafe as before. Badly hurt, they duck behind cover rather than just running
- **Enemies crouch behind low cover**: at a waist-high spot an enemy ducks down between bursts and stands up to shoot, so shots over the top miss it while it's down — catch it as it rises, or flank it. Hiding, it stays crouched the whole time, so badly hurt enemies can now hide behind counters and crates as well as walls. Crouched, it still peeks over the top and keeps watching you. Other enemies see the duck too. Animals and anything with *Can Crouch* off still stand
- **Flanking**: now and then an enemy circles round to your side instead of charging straight at you, taking the route you're least likely to see. It heads for where it believes you are, so moving while it's out of sight can leave it flanking an empty spot
- **Flanks end once they pay off**: a ranged enemy that has circled for at least a second and a half and now has you in view, in range and with a clear shot stops flanking and starts shooting, instead of finishing the walk
- **Eyes on the target while repositioning**: when flanking, moving between cover, falling back or retreating, an enemy keeps facing you (or where it last knew you were) and sidesteps or backpedals instead of turning its back, so it keeps seeing what it is dealing with. Anywhere else it still faces the way it walks
- **Hopping low cover**: in a generated level, enemies hop low walls, counters and barriers instead of walking all the way round — a quick hop up and over, a little slower than walking — wherever going round would take much longer. So low cover protects you from shots but not from being reached: a flanker can come straight over the divider you're crouched behind. They still walk round a lone crate, and never climb full-height walls
- **Searching cover**: an enemy hunting you favours the cover near where it lost you that would hide someone from it, before sweeping the rest of the area
- Cover follows whatever is built: the scan runs on the NavMesh, so generated levels get cover too

- **Bush approach**: enemies plan how to go into a bush they know instead of running to its middle. Sneaking through bushes, they cross the patch the way they are going — in at the edge they meet, out the far side — or clip its nearest part. A calm roamer stops a little way in from the side it came at. Breaking off to hide from a threat, an enemy picks the way in that keeps the bush between it and the threat, going round the outside to that side if going straight in would face the threat, and lies deep on the far side. Falling back to a known bush lies on the far side too. The spectator's ground overlay outlines each known bush as the patch the agent remembers
- **Hazard awareness**: enemies walk round damaging ground (rifts, snares, incendiary fires, lingering area effects) instead of through it, keeping to the same side of a zone once they have chosen it and turning aside before the edge if a zone opens across their path. Cover inside a hazard is never picked
- **Other threats count for cover**: besides the hostile it is taking cover from, an enemy weighs the other hostiles it can see, and prefers cover that also screens it from them

## Enemy Abilities & Reactions
- **Dodging**: an enemy under fire sometimes sidesteps out of the line of fire — a quick dash to whichever side is open — instead of standing in it. Not every time, and not more than every few seconds, so holding steady aim still pays
- **Grenades**: a ranged enemy that has just lost sight of you lobs a grenade onto where it thinks you are hiding, to flush you out — after a short wind-up you can see (and hear) coming. It won't throw at its own feet, needs a reachable arc, carries three, and waits between throws. Its grenades never hurt its own side. It aims for where the grenade will go off, not where it first lands: it allows for the bounce and the roll (throwing short on a slope or a bouncy floor, or lobbing more steeply so it rolls less), and won't make a throw that would bounce off a wall and end up far from you. If it sees you moving it leads you to where you'll be when the grenade lands — never further than the blast reaches, and never through a wall
- **Abilities**: enemies can use the same ability assets as the player (shockwaves, projectiles, targeted effects) with a cast time and cooldown of their own; abilities only the player can use simply aren't available to them
- Barks and animation cues can be played from a tree at any moment (a shout before a charge, a taunt animation)
- **Wandering**: instead of a fixed route, an enemy can drift between random spots around its post or wherever it stands

## Enemy Voices
- Enemies call out what they're doing, so you can read a fight by ear: spotting you, going to check a noise, hunting, losing you ("where'd they go?"), giving up, a kill, flanking, laying down suppressing fire, throwing a grenade, falling back and running for an alarm. The director adds "push!" and "hold position"
- A level's worth of enemies doesn't talk over itself: there's a short pause after any shout, the same call isn't repeated by anyone for a few seconds, and each enemy rests between shouts. The calls that matter most — spotted, grenade, falling back, alarm — always cut through
- Each enemy type has its own voice set, and some calls only come now and then (an investigation, a flank), so they stay meaningful. *No voice lines are assigned yet: every enemy uses a silent default set, ready for clips*

## Enemy Squads
- Enemies placed under a squad fight together. While fighting, the squad hands out roles every couple of seconds from who is closest: the nearest ranged member **pins you down** — firing at where it thinks you are from cover, whether it can see you or not — and the nearest melee members **go straight in**, while everyone else **flanks**. Flankers take turns going left and right, so two of them close in from both sides
- **Radio**: squad members hear each other's callouts across the whole area (about 40 m), walls or not — spot one and the rest know where you were seen
- **Leader**: the first member leads. When the leader goes down, everyone else's morale takes a hit — sometimes enough to make them fall back — and the next member takes over
- The ready-made trees follow the roles: melee flankers always flank on their side, and a ranged suppressor keeps firing from cover without needing a clear shot

## Enemy Behavior Trees
- Each enemy type's behavior tree decides what it does each moment; senses, attacks, stuns and death are handled for every tree the same way. One tree per type means one asset to read, tune and watch live
- Trees are built visually in the **Behavior Tree Editor** (CGD › AI › Behavior Tree Editor, or double-click a tree asset): drag nodes onto a canvas, connect them, and edit each node's settings in the side panel. Children run left to right and are numbered, nodes are coloured by kind (composite, decorator, condition, action), and each shows a one-line summary of its settings plus optional designer notes
- Editing works like the rest of Unity: undo/redo, copy/paste/duplicate, delete, a searchable create-node menu (Space or right-click), automatic tidy layout, pan and zoom
- **Live view**: while playing, select an enemy and its tree lights up — running nodes yellow, successes green, failures red, faded when they weren't checked this frame — next to the enemy's blackboard (its working memory)
- **Mistakes caught while editing**: every edit re-checks the tree and marks problems on the nodes (⛔ won't work, ⚠ probably not what you meant, ⓘ worth knowing): parents with nothing to run, nodes not connected to the root, subtrees with no tree or that loop back into themselves, branches that never finish with nothing able to interrupt them, abort modes with nothing to check, a branch that waits for a reason (Motive) but can't interrupt what runs after it, and blackboard keys that are read but never written (usually a typo). The toolbar counts them and jumps to the next one; with nothing selected, the side panel lists them all
- **Key picker**: every blackboard key field has a ▾ menu of the keys the enemy itself provides (Health, Morale…) and the keys this and other trees use, with their kind of value — no more typing names by memory
- **Groups and sticky notes** on the canvas to label areas ("Fight", "Routine") and explain a tree to whoever opens it next
- **Debugging** (in game, see *Spectator mode* for the same views without the editor): while watching an enemy, the side panel shows what it's doing and believes, its squad job, its melee turn, the director's phase and how many bold moves are in use (and whether it holds one), and how often it's thinking right now. A trace lists its recent decisions newest first — what started, succeeded, failed, and what interrupted what ("Patrol ⇥ interrupted by Fight"); click one to jump to the node. The selected node shows what it and each of its children last reported, marking the child that decided the outcome ("why didn't it attack?"). Breakpoints pause the game when a node starts or fails, for the watched enemy
- **Interruptions that read clearly**: Sequences and Selectors have an *abort mode*. A branch that aborts *lower priority* interrupts whatever is running to its right as soon as its opening conditions hold (spotting the player stops a patrol); one that aborts *self* stops as soon as its own conditions break (a target stepping out of reach stops an attack). Only quick checks are re-asked, never actions, and a branch behind a cooldown or limit doesn't interrupt only to give up
- **Subtrees**: a tree can run another tree in one node, sharing the same enemy and memory, so common behaviour (regrouping, raising the alarm, the idle routine) is built once and used by every enemy type. Double-click a subtree node to open it; the live view works inside it too
- **Variety**: a Chance decorator, a Weighted Random Selector (flank 1 : charge 3) and a Utility Selector, which picks whichever option scores best right now from the enemy's state — health, morale, awareness, distance to where it thinks you are — and switches when another clearly wins
- **Memory without code**: every enemy's blackboard shows its Health, Morale, Awareness, TargetDistance and whether it's on edge, plus the director's pacing phase, pressure and how stressed the player is, live; trees can also count things, remember positions and walk back to them

- **AI Simulator scene** (`Scenes/AISimulator`): a 60 × 44 m walled arena laid out for the three fighting ranges, with an alarm panel on the north wall. In the north is a **gallery**: a firing line of low crates to shoot over, with full-height bunkers behind it, and a 20–30 m view down the arena. The **middle ground** is open, with full-height stones about 6 m apart to dash between. Two **side lanes** run behind long walls, with a gap halfway, for getting round unseen. The **south yard**, where the player starts, has L-shaped walls and low crates for medium-range skirmishes. Full-height cover (white) hides whoever is behind it; low cover (grey) can be shot over and hides no one standing. Eight **concealment zones** (bush thickets for now; the same component also does smoke, and fog or similar areas would be more kinds) are spread over the arena (the middle, both side-lane doorways, the gallery, the south yard and the north corners): see-through green areas agents walk through, that hide whoever stands in them — they fade to a faint ghost so a spectator still sees them — from rivals further than a couple of metres, unless that rival is in the same zone. Attacking or casting from one gives the agent away for a moment. Two capsule agents per faction stand for that faction rather than any particular enemy (the second of each is named *Bio Agent 2* and so on; each pair forms a squad, so you can watch herd, union and coven behaviour), colour-coded green (BIO), yellow (TECH) and purple (VOID). The factions are hostile to each other as well as to the player, so with the player switched off they fight among themselves. They can see across the whole arena (60 m), and a fallen agent topples over and comes back 3 seconds later in its own faction's ground (around its patrol route), at the spot furthest from its rivals and the player and out of their sight where it can, so nobody respawns inside a rival's position or under its guns.
  - **BIO — close range**: a stalking predator that lives in the bushes. Calm, it roams from bush to bush round its territory, stopping in each to sniff and rest, and every Bio creature sneaks through bushes on the way wherever it travels unhurried (roaming, investigating, stalking, heading home) — up to 60% further than the direct way. Once it is hunting a target it is hard to turn away from it: another hostile has to be much more tempting to draw it off, the more worn down its prey the more it sees it through, and getting hit by someone else only distracts it a little. It hunts in a loop: it **stalks** it for a few seconds, circling at 8–12 m from bush to bush and watching it — cut short the moment the target turns its back or reloads — then **rushes** in for a committed strike (up to 4 s), **breaks off** at once to a bush at least 9 m from the target (or full cover, or just away), **lies low** there for a few seconds watching, and starts stalking again. Getting hurt still sends it into its Ambush, and it still dodges and Surges as before. Without a target in sight after a stalk it falls back on its old instincts: follow up where the target went, pounce, run down the wounded. It zig-zags on the way in when it sees the shot coming (you aiming squarely at it, or bullets cracking past) — reading your aim rather than flinching after a hit lands, pounces from about 14 m, and runs down anything at half health or less within 35 m. It strikes by pulling an arm back and thrusting it out
  - **TECH — mid range**: a siege engine that never lets up. It fights from cover around 13 m away, shooting 3-round bursts of slow, glowing yellow bolts that lead a moving target, then moves to a fresh spot every few seconds. If anything gets within about 6 m it falls back to cover further off. Once its shield breaks it ducks behind full-height cover until the shield recovers, and it throws a grenade when it just lost sight of its target. Defensive as it is, it doesn't camp the back line: with its target more than about 21 m off it pushes up a bound at a time (every 10 s at most) to cover closer in — never nearer than the 13 m it likes to fight at — or walks in a few metres when it knows no cover there. Given the suppress role it pins its target down only while it saw it moments ago (4 s), so it stops firing at a spot its target left long since — or that a Void minion vanished from when its time ran out. **Flushing out**: when its target goes to ground somewhere it saw it go — into a bush, or behind a wall or other solid cover — it doesn't walk round to look: for up to 10 s it keeps the pressure on, throwing a grenade on the spot every 7 s to drive it out into the open (the squad's suppressor keeps firing instead), firing into the spot while the sighting is fresh, a flanker bounding to fresh cover at another angle on it, and otherwise watching the spot for it to break cover; the moment the target shows itself it is back to shooting. In a squad, the lead is the anchor that pins the target and the others work the angles and throw the grenades
  - **VOID — the control mage**: it holds you at long range (about 24 m, strafing) and works spells instead of hiding or stabbing. When you are further than about 33 m off it first gets in range — circling round to a flank at most once, and its coven mates who are given the flank role do the same — and the moment you are inside that range it stops flanking and starts controlling: it keeps casting its slow violet bolt, drops a Void Rift on you when you hold still (spells reach about 30 m), drains you with its Siphon cone when you get within about 9 m, and backs off or slips to the side when you close in. It no longer plays peekaboo, creeps closer only while you look away, lunges when you flinch, stabs you in melee, or hides after casting; it still sidesteps fire now and then, goes intangible or Phase Shifts away when hurt, hides while its shield recovers and comes at a target that broke contact from the side. Its eerie flicker while you stare at it stays. Its bolt is a fat projectile that bursts 1.6 m wide, and it knows it: it won't fire when the bolt's width would clip a wall or half cover on the way, and it only fires at a target behind cover when the blast would still reach it, so it repositions to a clear line instead of throwing bolts into the wall

  - **Archetypes**: TECH is robotic and defensive: ranged, always in cover, it never sidesteps incoming fire — it digs in, suppresses and lobs grenades. When it must fall back it follows a plan: it picks cover it knows that screens it from its target, is well away from every enemy it senses (not just the one it fights), is reached without running past any of them or across the target's view, and is near its home ground; it re-plans as enemies move, and only runs blindly if it knows no such spot. BIO is organic and offensive: melee, instinct-driven, charging in, dashing out only to heal. VOID is haunting and chaotic: magic at medium range, erratic — it nearly always sidesteps fire (about every second), keeps circling to new angles every few seconds, and leaves frail minions and rifts in its wake
  - **Range roles**: each faction spreads its squad over the whole fight by giving attackers, tanks and supports different ranges — *close* (within about 8 m), *mid* (about 8–18 m) and *long* (over 18 m). **BIO**: the attacker closes in (it pounces), the tank holds mid range (about 11 m) and the support stays far back (about 24 m). **TECH**: the support holds the line up close, the attacker fights at mid range (about 13 m) and the tank, a siege engine, shells from long range (about 35 m). **VOID**: the tank walks in at close range, the support casts from mid range (about 13 m) and the attacker works spells from long range (about 24 m). An enemy's range is where a ranged one holds (its Preferred Range) or just past where a melee one swings; behaviors shared between roles, such as the Tech and Void *stances* that tanks and supports fall back on, size their distances from it (Target In Range can be set as a share of the engagement range), so the same behavior suits a close, mid or long range fighter
  - **Support agents**: each faction also has a support agent (*Tech Support*, *Bio Support*, *Void Support*) with its own focused tree and its own role; the three faction agents keep the full trees that try out every behavior. Each stands where it helps most — within reach of as many of its allies as it can, in cover facing the threat when it knows some, moving with the line every couple of seconds as the fight shifts — breaks off to help whoever is being hit or worn down, and when calm follows its squad's leader and patches up anyone still hurt. *Void Support* (voodoo enchanter) works the fight from far back: it keeps about 8 m behind the ally nearest the threat (a mid-range 13 m or so from its target, behind its tank and in front of its long-range attackers) and spends it enchanting and cursing — *Void Empowerment* over every ally within 12 m (+25% damage, shields recharge 50% faster for 6 s), *Void Ward* on whoever is being hit (+30 shield and +12 health), *Curse* on its target (a little true damage and three stacks of Curse) and, every 14 s, a *Curse Totem* thrown onto the target: a destructible totem (90 health, anyone can shoot it down; its side can't) that stands up to 14 s in a 4.5 m zone which adds a Curse stack and 2 true damage to every enemy in it each second. Its basic attack is a low true-damage bolt (12) that adds a Curse stack 30% of the time, only filling the time between casts. When anything gets within 7 m it phases out with *Wrong Echo*: for 5 s it is untouchable and walks through walls. *Tech Support* (shield wall) fights behind a front-facing barrier: a translucent wall of force in front of it that stops everything coming at its front — shots and projectiles, and also blasts, gas and damage zones, melee swings and beams: nobody behind the wall is hurt by a hit from its front, and the Tech Support itself takes no damage from hits within 70° in front of it — while its own and its allies' shots from behind pass straight through. A blast or blow from behind or beside the wall (a grenade lobbed over it to land behind, an attacker who has flanked round) lands as usual. Heavily plated, it picks its spot for the job and rethinks it every few seconds rather than holding one place: whenever it knows where the threat is it stands between the threat and its allies, on the line from the threat to them (about 5 m ahead of the one nearest the threat, facing it, behind a wide 3.2 m wall, with no cover but its own wall), close enough that its allies are within reach of its repairs and buffs; when it has lost sight it waits in cover facing the threat, within reach of its allies; to repair someone it goes to them. It never falls back while the wall holds. It never opens fire, but zones the front: anything that comes within 7 m of it it steps out to meet, shield first, and sears with the *blowtorch* it repairs allies with (a short flame melee strike that sets the target burning), then goes back to its spot; it bashes away anything that rushes it and Fortifies when it takes fire. When allies within 10 m are being hit it casts *Barrier Field* over them (+60 armour for 6 s, every 20 s) without lowering its wall. Every 14 s it lobs a *Barrier Dome* onto the squadmate taking the most fire (or, failing that, the one nearest the threat): a sphere of force that stops hostile shots and projectiles coming in from outside for 9 s (or until it has stopped 400 damage) while shots fired from inside fly out; blasts, melee and beams ignore it. It only goes to help allies that are badly worn (half or less). With no allies near it keeps to cover and only sears what reaches it. To repair it lowers the wall: it pops a *Smoke Screen* where it stands, then casts *Field Restoration* (+28 shield and +14 plating to everyone within 8 m, itself included), on itself when worn down or after walking over to an ally that is; the wall is back up once the cast is done. Focused fire breaks the wall (it staggers, and the wall is gone for 6 s): it smokes and drops back behind its allies until it is up again. *Bio Support* (spore mender) is the pack's long-range support: it keeps far behind the pack, some 24 m from the target, slipping there through the bushes, and works from afar. It lobs *Mending Spores* onto whoever is being mauled or worn down (an ally at 55% or less): a 5 m cloud thrown up to 20 m, healing 30 and regrowing 12 bone plating for everyone in it every 7 s and clearing burns, bleeds, poison, chills and slows; roars the whole pack on with *Rallying Roar* (22 m) and spits slow bone spikes at the target between casts (14 damage). Anything that gets within 7 m of it is met with a *Feeding Bite* lunge (every 14 s): a 14-damage bite that latches on and holds the target for up to 3 s — the player must tap Interact to *shake it off* (an enemy is stunned for the duration) — while it drains the target (5 damage every half second, armour and shield soaking it like any hit) and heals itself for 60% of the damage it takes, the bite included; the drain stops the moment the player breaks free. The bite also leaves a bleed that outlasts the hold (6 s). Once it lets go it backs away to its range again. It only slips away when badly hurt and cornered, and with no pack near it simply holds its distance and spits. The *Support* lineup in the AI Simulator puts two agents and one support agent in each faction's squad
  - **Tank agents**: Bio and Void also have a tank (*Bio Tank*, *Void Tank*) that leads its squad from the front: slow, far harder to kill than an agent and resistant to its own faction's effects, low on damage but built to pressure the enemy and soak what would hit its team. A tank that has not seen anything itself walks up toward whatever a squadmate within 40 m is fighting (stopping about 15 m short) until it sees the enemy too, rather than idling beside whoever started the fight. It also lobs Acid Spew pools into the enemy's path, chokes anything that gets within 5 m in a Plague Cloud, poisons with every blow in reach and regrows 15 health a second for 8 s once below 60%; poison doesn't touch it. *Tech Tank* (a sentinel, not a front-line bruiser) is a slow, heavily plated machine that keeps its own post just behind the rest of the Tech, about 4 m behind the ally nearest the enemy next to cover, close enough that, like the other tanks, it takes 30% of every hit an ally within 8 m suffers onto its own plating and shield, and makes cover useless. For now it has no abilities at all, only its basic attack: no Mortar Shell, Rocket, Smoke Mortar, Flamethrower or Weapon Bash. Its basic attack is a three-step combo, one step per attack: an inaccurate burst of five bullets (7 damage each, 6° of spread), then three small mortar rounds lobbed half a second apart that land a few metres off (impact grenades, 16 damage in a 2.2 m blast, 7° of scatter), then a missile cannon blast (22 damage in a 3 m blast); it carries no ordinary grenade. Each step has a cast time (0.6, 0.8 and 1 s) and then a decompress (1.5, 1.5 and 1.8 s) during which it can move but does nothing except reposition: it heads for the best spot just behind its allies, next to cover facing the enemy (alone, it keeps its range for a moment). It weaves its shots into its movement, a little like kiting: it charges and fires the bullet burst and the mortar strikes while still walking toward its spot, and only stands still to charge and fire the missile; after each step it keeps repositioning while it decompresses and fires the next step on the move the moment it is ready; its post shifts only every few seconds rather than with every step its allies take. After the missile it waits 2.6 s before starting over; a combo it can't continue within 8 s starts again from the bullets. When it has no squad to hold a post for, or its role has nothing to do, it fights the way the TECH agent does: backs off to long-range cover when something gets within 9 m, otherwise fires from cover for a few seconds, then moves to a fresh spot (or keeps its distance and fires on the move with no cover). Otherwise it fires its combo from its post. The Tech Tank is also the anchor of its squad: Tech agents and the Tech Support keep within about 14 m of it, choosing cover near it and walking back to it when they stray (the squad's leader and a calm explorer may range a few metres further), so the squad holds one area round the siege engine. The tank takes ground slowly in return by following its allies — it keeps its post just behind the squad's front, so as the squad takes ground the siege tank moves up with it — and when calm it trails its squad's leader closely instead of waiting for it to get far ahead. A Tech squad without a tank behaves as before. The other factions gather round their tank in their own looser way: the Bio herd keeps within about 22 m of the plague hulk and the Void coven within about 20 m of the frost colossus when calm — agents and supports that drift further close up on it again, slipping through the bushes (Bio) or along the walls (Void), so the pack moves at the tank's slow pace — while in a fight the Bio predators and Void skirmishers keep their own roles (pouncing, flanking, casting from long range) and only prefer cover near it. Tanks and supports that find a squadmate fighting something they haven't seen now walk up to it, and when nothing is happening they follow their squad's leader or go about their routine instead of standing still (the join-the-fight step used to hold them up in calm, a bug) The *Tank* lineup in the AI Simulator puts the tank at the head of the Bio and Void squads and the Tech Tank at the back of the Tech squad
  - **Who they talk to**: calls for help only ever pass between kin — the same faction, plus any faction it has a pact with (none by default). TECH works like a union: every Tech that sees a target shares it with all the others every second over a long-range link (80 m, through walls), each leans toward the target its mates are already fighting, and in a squad one pins the target down with suppressing fire while the others bound to new firing positions for a crossfire. VOID works as a coven: like the union it shares every sighting over the link and leans toward the target its kin fight, and its encounter's deepest member presses on while the rest work round the target's side (its Phase Shift minions are loners that neither call nor answer). BIO runs in herds: it stays close to its kin, a straggler cut off from the herd (nothing of its kind within 25 m) rejoins it before pouncing, a calm one drifts back to the herd instead of wandering off, and in a squad some of the herd circle to the target's side while the rest go straight in. A lone agent behaves as before — these only show with two or more of a faction
  - **Where did it go? (belief)**: once an agent loses sight of its target it no longer reverts to a generic search. It weighs the cover spots and bushes round where it last saw it — likelier the further it could have got in the time that has passed, and ahead of the way it was heading — crosses off the ones it can see are empty, and works through the rest in order. Nobody can see into a bush from afar, so a bush is only crossed off once the agent has walked up to it, and TECH doesn't pick a vantage to watch bushes from. Each faction does it in character: TECH takes up a vantage that overlooks the likeliest spots and holds there watching, then bounds to the next; BIO runs straight to the likeliest spot, checks it and carries on down the route; VOID drifts to places picked at random in proportion to how likely they are, so it feels haunting rather than predictable. Noises and calls for help give it a weaker lead. The belief fades over about 30 seconds, after which the old search takes over. It never reads the target's real position while it is out of sight
  - **Choosing whom to fight**: with more than one hostile in view each faction picks its target in its own way, and it no longer ignores someone else shooting it mid-fight. BIO is a predator: it goes for the most badly hurt target in reach. TECH shoots back at whoever is shooting it, but works methodically: the more it has worn its current target down, the more it weighs seeing it through, so a fresh attacker pulls it away early in a fight but not off a target it has nearly broken. VOID is an opportunist: it picks on whoever is busy fighting someone else. All of them stick with their current target unless another is clearly better
  - **Losing sight**: each faction now stays in the fight for a while after its target slips out of view, instead of wandering straight off to search. TECH holds its cover (or takes some) and watches the corner the target vanished behind for about 12 seconds before going to look. BIO circles round toward where it went, then goes to ground behind full cover facing that way, ready to pounce, until about 10 seconds have passed. VOID keeps flanking toward the last known position and stands watching in between
  - **BIO smells blood**: a target within 35 m that is down to half its health and shield or less is run down straight away, with no cover-to-cover dashes
  - **Damaging zones**: agents know when they are standing in a hostile zone such as a Void Rift and weigh stepping out against staying: the harder it hurts and the more hurt they already are, the sooner they leave, while a target close to dying can be worth soaking a little more damage to finish. They step out to the nearest clear ground, keeping their eyes on their target, and fight on from there. Their own side's zones don't bother them. They also path round zones that would hurt them instead of walking in and stepping straight back out, and head for a destination inside one (a target standing in a rift) only as far as its edge; when a zone fills the only way through, they go through it
  - **Faction abilities** (one each, on a cooldown). Being ready is not a reason to cast: each agent weighs the situation, and the stronger and more numerous its reasons, the sooner it acts (being hurt, being hit hard, being shot at, its shield worn down, how close the target is, a target holding still, several hostiles near). One strong reason or a few weak ones together will do. With none, most abilities still get used, but only after the agent has held off for a while; Ambush is never used without a reason. The moment its reasons add up (or its patience runs out) an ability cuts into whatever the agent is doing; abilities on cooldown or without a reason never interrupt:
    - **VOID — Phase Shift** (every 18 s, disengage and sustain): after a half-second wind-up it vanishes and reappears up to 15 m away behind full-height cover that neither the other factions nor the player can see, popping back into view there; for 5 s its shield then recharges three times as fast. Two frail Void minions (15 health, faster than any agent, melee only: they stab for 5) appear beside it where it reappears and go straight after its target: for their first 5 s they know where that target is and go for it over anyone else (unless another hostile is right on top of them), then pick their own targets, guarding it while it recovers for 15 s, or until they or it fall. A minion that dissolves is gone for whoever was fighting it: they stop shooting at, searching for or believing in it at once, as they would for one that died. They rush with no thought for their own survival (no dodging, regrouping or backing off), but not blindly: still some way off and in their target's view, they dash to the next cover closer to it or swing round its side by the least visible route, and only sprint straight in once close or out of its sight. It shifts to escape and recover, and does so before it gets killed: whatever else it is doing, it shifts out once its shield is mostly gone and it keeps taking damage, or once its health itself is dropping, and sooner still when it is being shot at or crowded. Dropping below half health, it shifts away before trying to run for cover. Without any reason it holds it for up to 40 s. Rivals lose it and go looking where they last saw it. With nowhere hidden to go it doesn't try, and runs for cover instead
    - **TECH — Fortify** (every 18 s, zone control): digs in for 8 s — +60 armour on top of its 100 (damage taken drops from half to under 40%) and 30 extra shield, charged the moment it digs in and gone when Fortify ends, at half speed — and spends them laying down suppressive fire on its target, seen or hiding, with a grenade every 4 s. It digs in when under fire or taking damage, with its shield worn down, as the target comes into mid range, or with several hostiles near; otherwise only after holding off for about 25 s. Fortified, it no longer stops to sidestep incoming fire, but still falls back if something gets within 9 m
    - **BIO — Surge** (every 16 s, offensive sustain and damage): for 8 s it goes berserk: it moves 30% faster, swings 60% faster and heals by half of the damage it deals (no regeneration). While it lasts it does nothing but run its target down and keep hitting it — no stalking, breaking off, hiding or Ambushing — and it fights on even if its nerve breaks, living off the lifesteal. It surges mostly as it closes in on its target (the brawl is where it pays off), sooner when it needs the lifesteal (hurt or being hit), when the target is weak, or with several hostiles near
    - **VOID — Void Rift** (every 14 s, zone and sustain): drops a 4 m rift on its target that lasts 6 s, preferably on a target holding still, when it has shield to siphon back, and closer in. Everything hostile inside takes a little damage every half second (armour doesn't help) and is chilled with Cryo — slowed 15% and losing 5% of its armour a stack, up to 4 stacks — and Void takes three quarters of the damage dealt back as shield, wherever it is. Standing in a rift for long is a losing trade
  - **Reading body language**: every enemy now watches how its target moves while it can see it: whether its back is turned or it is reloading (an opening), how long it has been stared at squarely, and whether the target just whipped round in a panic. Each faction uses this in its own way (below). An unseen target gives nothing away
  - **BIO hunts like a predator**:
    - **Primal Roar** (every 35 s): when its target has gone to ground, it is below 60% health or two or more hostiles are close, BIO roars. After a 0.7 s cast it knows where every hostile in its territory is — through walls, bushes and cloaks — for 8 s, and is tougher (+60% max health, filled) and hits harder (+50% damage) for the same 8 s
    - **Barbed Snare** (every 14 s): BIO bursts a 3 m patch of barbed growth under a target more than 6 m away, mostly one that is moving fast or getting away. For 5 s everything hostile in it is slowed (up to 40%) and starts to bleed, and the patch squelches the first time each one steps in, so the rest of the herd hears the trap spring. BIO then circles while the trap does its work. Rivals step out of it as they would from a rift
    - **Acid Spew** (every 10 s): with its target in view between 5 and 18 m, BIO spits a green ball of acid at it in a lob. It bursts the moment it hits anything, hurting everything hostile within 3 m for 20 and poisoning them, and leaves a 3 m pool of acid for 5 s that keeps hurting and poisoning whatever stands in it (poison stacks, so lingering in the pool gets worse fast). Rivals step out of the pool and route round it as they would a rift. BIO then carries on its hunt. It spits at the start of each round of its hunt (stalk, rush, break off), never in the middle of a rush. If its target ducks into a bush or behind cover within 18 m, BIO stops stalking the empty spot and spits acid onto where it went to ground, so the pool drives it back out
    - **Venom Bite** (every 8 s): with its target in view between 3 and 7 m, BIO crouches for a moment (the tell) and lunges at it, stopping just in front of it. It bites whatever is in front of it where it lands, for 22 damage and poison. Step aside during the crouch and the bite lands on air. The lunge cuts into its stalk or rush the moment the target comes into range, and walls and drops stop it short
    - **Bone Spike** (every 9 s): with its target in view between 4 and 20 m, BIO winds up for half a second and fires a bone spike at it, aimed ahead of a target on the move, out to about 22 m. The spike passes through up to three bodies in its way before the next one stops it. Walls stop it straight away. Everyone it hits takes 15 damage and starts to bleed, so a line of enemies, or a target behind its friends, all bleed together. Like Acid Spew, it fires between rounds of the hunt, never in the middle of a rush
    - **Scent**: BIO can smell blood. A bleeding target (from the snare or anything else) can be tracked for up to 30 m through walls, the way footsteps are heard: it knows roughly where you are going but never exactly where you are, and the trail ends when the bleed does
    - **Pounce on an opening**: turn your back on a BIO within 18 m, or reload in front of it, and it charges at once
    - **Stared down**: hold a BIO squarely in your view for a second and a half while it is still out of reach and it won't charge into the stare; it breaks off and circles round to come at you from where you're not looking (at most every 8 s). Facing it down buys time
  - **TECH runs protocols**:
    - **Weapon Bash** (every 8 s): with someone within 3 m, TECH smashes them with its weapon for 20 damage in a short cone in front of it. A bashed target is dazed: slowed to a fifth of its speed, easing back to normal over 3 s. TECH then falls back (to cover, or just away) while the slow holds
    - **Shotgun Blast** (every 7 s): with someone in view within 8 m, TECH fires a blast of 8 pellets in a tight cone (5 damage each, so up to 40 if all land). Every pellet that hits has a 10% chance to set the target alight (a short burn), so the closer you are and the more pellets land, the likelier you burn. The pellets fly as visible yellow bolts out to 14 m and can be dodged. TECH then falls back as it does with anyone too close
    - **Smoke Screen** (every 16 s): when TECH is being watched and has reason to break contact (its shield worn down, taking damage, shot at, hurt), it bursts a smoke cloud where it stands, shown as a single round smoke-grey sphere. It dashes half way to the cloud's edge in a random direction, so whoever was watching loses it in the smoke and can't tell which way it went. The cloud hides it and blocks sight lines like a thrown smoke grenade, and casting it doesn't give TECH away. TECH then falls back, unseen, to cover it knows screens it from its target
    - **Thruster Dash** (every 10 s): with someone within 6 m, TECH turns its back on them and dashes 7 m away (less if a wall or a drop is in the way), leaving a strip of burning floor along the whole path. The dash only goes the way TECH is facing, so it has to turn round first, and that half-second turn is the tell. For 4 s the strip hurts anything hostile standing in it a little every half second and sets it alight. Rivals step out of it and route round it, so it also cuts off a chase
    - **Survey Beacon** (every 25 s): four seconds after losing sight of its target, TECH launches a beacon (a small glowing yellow post) toward where the target was. For 15 s it pulses every 3 s with a visible ring. With hostiles within 12 m it strips their stealth (bushes and cloaks) for a moment and reports the nearest one to every TECH within 80 m, through walls. When it finds no one, it tops up the shields of TECH standing near it. It has 60 health and anyone but TECH can shoot it down, including other factions. Hold Interact on it for 2 s to spoof it: it turns grey, stops revealing anyone, and from then on reports hostiles in the wrong place, sending TECH to search the far side of the beacon
  - **VOID leans into dread**:
    - **Echo** (every 30 s): under pressure (shot at, hurt, or with the target closing in), VOID turns intangible for 5 s. Nothing hurts it and walls are nothing to it: it heads straight for where it is going through whatever is in the way, in plain view the whole time, and it can still attack and use abilities. When the time is up it finishes walking out of any wall it is in before it becomes solid again (shots that hit it are simply lost)
    - **Siphon** (every 14 s): with its target in view within 9 m, VOID channels for a moment (0.9 s) and then drains everyone in a 70° cone up to 10 m in front of it for 26 damage each, healing itself for 125% of the damage it actually dealt
    - **Curse** (every 20 s): with its target in view and reason to (mostly a target in heavy armour, also one in range of its bolts or worth finishing), VOID gathers itself for half a second and lays three stacks of *Curse* on it, with a little true damage. While cursed, a share of every hit the target takes — from VOID or anyone else — lands as true damage, which armour does nothing against (shields still absorb it): 8% per stack, up to 50% at five stacks, lasting 8 s with the stacks wearing off one at a time. Void's bolts add a stack now and then (40% of the time) and Void Slash two, so a long fight builds it up. Against a Fortified TECH it is the difference between scratching it and wearing it down
    - **Void Slash** (every 6 s): with someone within 3 m, VOID rakes them with a short slash in a 120° arc in front of it for 30 true damage — armour does nothing against it — that also curses them (two stacks) — before it gets back to keeping its distance
    - **Frost Charge** (every 12 s): with its target in view between 6 and 20 m, VOID lobs a violet charge at it that goes off twice where it comes to rest. The first blast, 1.5 s after the throw, hurts everything hostile within 4 m a little (12 at the centre, half at the edge) and chills them, slowing them. The charge then stays put, blinking icy blue, and 1.2 s later goes off again in the same spot, harder (28 at the centre) with a deeper chill that slows far more. Rivals who see it coming scramble out of range of both blasts. Anyone still in the circle for the second blast is badly slowed. If its target ducks into a bush or behind cover between 6 and 20 m away, VOID lobs a charge onto where it went to ground, so it comes back out chilled or sits through the harder freeze. VOID won't throw at a target closer than 6 m, and a throw it can't make doesn't use up its turn
    - **Punishing the flinch**: whipping round in a panic gives VOID a 2 s opening. If the target is within 16 m, VOID dives in for a stab. Calm, aimed checks give it nothing
  - **Ability effects** (simple placeholders): while casting, an agent holds its arm out in front for a physical move, but for a VOID spell (Rift, Null Field, Siphon, Ward, Empowerment, Curse, Glacial Shell, Echo, Phase Shift) it raises it upright like a wand, leaning toward its target and rocking as the spell builds (Void Slash, Frost Lash and Frost Slam keep the forward thrust). Each cast goes off with a flash in the faction's colour, and for as long as a buff lasts the agent wears a pulsing translucent shell in that colour that fades in and out. Fortified TECH also has a glowing yellow ring planted at its feet; surging BIO gives off a small green pulse each time it heals off a hit, and a hidden BIO is mostly see-through. VOID's Phase Shift bursts violet where it vanished and again where it reappears (as it pops back into view), then wears its violet shell while its shield refills; its Void Rift flashes as it casts and leaves a pulsing violet disc on the ground. Abilities that hit a fixed area show it as they go off, in the ability's colour: cone attacks spray faint streams across the whole cone (the Tech siege's Flamethrower in orange, the Void Tank's Frost Lash in ice blue, Void Slash in violet), and blasts and bursts round the caster swell to their full reach (Frost Slam, Plague Cloud, and very faintly the team heals and rallies), so you can see exactly what each one reaches. Every ability an agent uses now has at least a cast flash
  - **Damage numbers between factions**: every hit an agent lands shows a damage number on its victim, in the attacker's colour — green from BIO, yellow from TECH, violet from VOID (the player's own hits stay white) — so in a three-way fight you can see who is hurting whom. Critical hits are still bold and larger. They also show in spectator mode, and keep working when you switch between the Player and the spectator view
  - **Shot and attack effects**: TECH's yellow bolts are capsule-shaped (flying point first) and leave a fading yellow trail and a stream of sparks, and burst where they land; its grenades (yellow-bodied, blinking yellow) trail sparks through the air and go off in a yellow flash. VOID's bolts leave a thin violet trail with sparks that float upward and burst violet on impact. TECH and VOID flash at the muzzle each time they fire. BIO's arm leaves a green claw trail through each thrust, slashes green in front of it as the blow lands (not when a stagger spoils it), and bursts green on whatever it hits
  - **VOID third-parties**: when its target is fighting another faction, VOID slips round behind it while its back is turned (every 4 s at most)
  - Everyone sidesteps out of the line of fire now and then when shot at. Hiding means full-height cover only: a low crate no longer counts as out of sight for anyone. The player starts with a rifle and respawns on death
- **Sight cones in the Scene view**: every enemy's view cone (sight range and angle) is drawn on the ground. In play mode it turns yellow, orange or red as the enemy grows suspicious, searches or engages, with a line to whoever it is sensing; the selected enemy's cone stands out. It can be hidden from the Scene view's Gizmos menu
- **Faction doctrine**: each faction carries itself in its own way, in generated levels as well as the AI Simulator arena (the three tiers of every faction and the AI Simulator agents; Void's minions are left alone). Nothing here changes what an enemy can do in a fight, only how its nerve, its idle hours and its warning signs look:
  - **Nerve (morale)**:
    - *TECH* is steadied by numbers and wears down when it counts the odds: it loses nerve for every hostile beyond its kin close by, and each kin within 20 m steadies it. Seeing an officer (a higher tier) fall hurts more than a rank-and-file loss, but not hugely. When it breaks it falls back in good order to the nearest kin that still has its nerve (its squad leader first), at a measured pace, and calls everyone to where it is falling back from. Finding a fallen comrade is reported over the radio, sending its kin to look
    - *BIO* breaks late and mostly over its alpha: its own wounds shake it less (it is more dangerous hurt), it fights on while its target is weak (smelling victory it regains nerve in the fight), and herd mates nearby steady it. Seeing a stronger kin fall (an alpha) shakes a lesser one badly, while a weaker kin falling barely registers. Its apex — a tier 3 (or its boss) — holds the pack together: see one fall and every lesser Bio that saw it breaks and scatters home, so taking down the apex first can end a fight. When it finally breaks it runs home to its post. Its roar carries a good deal further than anyone else's
    - *VOID* barely cares for the fallen (they are expendable), but it cares about its shield: with the shield gone its nerve drains steadily, and it recovers from any shock very quickly. When shaken it drifts away to cover slowly, keeping its eyes on you, rather than running. Its call carries a shorter way
  - **Idle routines** (before they see you): standing watch and at stops on its route, each faction works through its own routine, so you can tell who is in a room before it knows you are there. On edge (after a fight or a fruitless search) the routine tightens or slackens in character:
    - *TECH* runs a drill, the same steps in the same order: a steady sweep of its view from side to side, a still pause to report in (it mutters, if it has a voice line), then a tight sweep, and round again. It stops at every waypoint to run a step, never halts at random and never leaves its post. It walks at an even pace. On edge its steps are shorter and its sweeps faster
    - *BIO* is restless and follows its mood: it sniffs about with quick glances over a wide arc, settles to rest for a long moment, or prowls a few metres from its post and back. It stops at waypoints only now and then, walks at an uneven pace and halts for a moment between them. On edge it is twitchier, with shorter spells of everything
    - *VOID* is wrong in small ways: it stares at one spot for a long while and then looks somewhere else with no turn in between, stands whispering, turns its back on the room and stays that way, or drifts a few metres from its post. It walks slowly with a drifting pace and stops dead mid-route. On edge it grows more patient, not less: its stares last longer
  - **Thinking ahead** (what they expect rather than what they see; a lead never counts on a target walking through a wall, and only a target in sight has a known speed):
    - *Cutting you off*: a chaser runs to where a moving target will be, not where it is. *BIO* is the best at it, leading you by most of the time it needs to reach you (up to 7 m), so running in a straight line from a Scavenger does not work; *VOID* leads you by about half, *TECH* barely at all
    - *Aiming ahead*: abilities that take a moment to go off (Void's Rift, Bio's Snare) are aimed at where a moving target will be when they land, not where it was. *VOID* expects you to keep running and leads furthest, *TECH* leads exactly, *BIO* is the least exact. A target standing still is aimed at where it stands
    - *Reading the route*: an enemy walking to somewhere it has not seen yet no longer takes the route blindly. *TECH* holds at each corner for half a second, looking down the next stretch, before rounding it (slicing the pie); *BIO* rushes long clear stretches at a quarter more speed and cuts the corners; *VOID* stops dead now and then to listen
  - **Fighting as a group** (encounters): enemies of one faction fighting the same target now act as a group on their own, in generated levels as well as the AI Simulator arena (before, only enemies placed in a squad did, and generated levels had none). Each group has a lead and hands out jobs; a lone enemy fights as before:
    - *Who leads*: *TECH* follows its chain of command (the highest tier, then whoever has been in the fight longest); *BIO* follows strength (tier and health together, so a fresh tier 2 takes over from a badly hurt tier 3); *VOID* follows depth (the highest tier, then whoever has most of its ward left)
    - *Jobs* (as in F.E.A.R.): *TECH* hands out slots for a siege: the lead anchors it, pinning the target down with suppressing fire from cover, and up to two already off to the target's side work crossfire angles (bounding to fresh cover every few seconds, and throwing the grenades that flush a hiding target out); the rest do the same. *BIO*: the alpha and the next strongest go straight in while the rest of the pack circles to the target's sides. *VOID*: the deepest presses on while the rest of the coven works round the target's side every few seconds. Jobs stay with whoever holds them unless someone is clearly better placed, so they don't flicker
    - *When the lead falls* (as in Halo, but nobody flees): the one next in line steps up — its nerve steadies, it takes the lead's job, and the mark over its head climbs and swells as it takes charge (it pulses three times) — while the rest regroup for a while. *TECH* re-forms (8 s): the new commander still takes point while every other gun pins the target down. *BIO* (6 s): the new alpha goes in alone while the pack circles round it. *VOID* (10 s): the coven drops its jobs and each member falls back on its own judgement, warier than usual. The successor is bolder than usual for a while (10–15 s)
  - **Each faction's own director** (overseer, after Alien: Isolation): every faction has something watching over all its members that knows where hostiles are, and uses it sparingly:
    - *Menace*: it builds while the faction's members are in contact and drains while they aren't. *VOID* pulls the whole faction back when it peaks (after about 18 s of one member in contact), and holds back for at least 12 s and until the menace has drained, so its hunt breathes in and out. *BIO* rests after a long brawl (later, and shorter). *TECH* never lets up
    - *Hints*: once its members have lost all contact for a while (VOID 20 s, TECH 30 s, BIO 35 s) it sends the calm member nearest a hostile to check a rough area around it — never the spot itself: VOID's hints are the vaguest (within 14 m), BIO's are 10 m, and TECH's network is the sharpest (6 m) and sends two members at once. Not while it is holding back
    - *Escalation*: every time a target gets away from one of its searches (at most once every 20 s) it escalates, up to three times a level, and its hints get sharper each time
  - **Temperament**: each faction weighs its options with a temperament (BIO aggressive and careless, TECH cautious, VOID cunning), and its situation moves it: regrouping or held back by its overseer it grows warier, a member who just stepped up grows bolder. VOID's choice between backing off, diving in and skirmishing now leans on it
  - **Getting out of the way**: an enemy that sees a hostile grenade within reach of it gets clear of the blast before it goes off (one behind a wall, where the blast can't reach it, stays put; a grenade from its own side is ignored):
    - *TECH* reacts almost at once, shouts a warning if it has a voice line, and takes the nearest spot outside the blast with a margin, preferring to get behind something solid
    - *BIO* is off before it thinks, at a sprint and well beyond the radius, without looking for cover
    - *VOID* is slow to register, then drifts out unhurried with its eyes on the grenade, content to stand at the edge and take a graze
  - **Fights start readably**: each faction shows the moment it notices something, the moment it commits, and the moment it stands down. The mark over its head moves in the faction's own way and takes a tint of its colour, and the enemy pulses in that colour:
    - *TECH*: a quick wobble of the "?" like a scanner as it notices; the "!" punches in crisply as it locks on, and it holds fire for a quarter of a second after deciding to fight
    - *BIO*: the "?" bobs as it tests the air; it hits the "!" with a pumped, shaking roar and charges with no delay at all
    - *VOID*: it snaps round to look and the "?" swells slowly and flickers, taking the longest to take it in; it stares for most of a second before it strikes
    - A second look at someone it was fighting a few seconds ago takes less than half the time
  - **After a fight** (awareness no longer switches off): an enemy that wins a fight stays on edge for a while, where before it relaxed at once. It stands over its kill in its own way for the pause: *TECH* scans round it in a sweep, *BIO* sniffs about it a good while, *VOID* stares at it unblinking for the longest. Then it stays on edge for a time that suits it (TECH the longest, VOID in between, BIO the shortest; a fruitless search leaves it on edge longer than a won fight, TECH most of all). On edge it notices faster, patrols quicker and glances about more. When it finally relaxes it shows it: TECH gives a quiet all-clear blink, BIO's tension goes out of it, VOID simply fades and loses interest. The "…" mark appears over its head
- **Faction teams**
- **Faction teams**: an enemy can be put on its faction's own team (BIO, TECH or VOID) instead of the shared enemy side; it then fights the other factions as well as the player. Only the AI Simulator scene's agents use this for now — in generated levels all factions still fight together against the player

## Enemy Design Tools
Making and tuning enemies without code — every tool is under **CGD › AI**:
- **Behaviors**: any branch of a tree (an ambush, a pounce, prowling when stared down, leaving a hazard) can be saved as a *behavior* — right-click its top node in the Behavior Tree Editor › *Save Branch as Behavior…*, or *Save as Behavior…* in the Behavior Composer. It moves into its own asset in the behavior library and the tree runs it from there, behaving exactly as before; other enemy types can then use the same behavior, so a Bio agent's ambush can be given to a Void enemy. Its comment becomes its description. A behavior can be **switched off** in one tree (it is skipped, as if absent) and back on, without rewiring anything
- **Behavior Composer** (CGD › AI › Behavior Composer, or *Behavior Composer* in an enemy's inspector): an enemy type's behaviors as plain lists instead of a graph — the top list (most urgent first) and the fight list, each in the order they are tried. Each behavior has an on/off switch, ▲ ▼ to change its priority, *Open* to edit it, *Copy* to change it for this enemy alone, and ✕ to remove it; *+ Add Behavior* opens a searchable list of the library by category — or *New Empty Behavior…* to write one from scratch (it is added to the list and opens in the Behavior Tree Editor) — and puts the new behavior above any catch-all that takes every turn (like the routine at the bottom), so it actually gets to run. Branches still written into the tree show too, with *Save as Behavior…*. *New Starter Tree…* makes a tree laid out like the AISimulator agents' (regroup, raise alarm, an empty fight list, the routine) to fill from the library. The library is searchable and lists every behavior with its description, when it acts, the kit it needs and how many trees use it
  - Every behavior says **when it acts** in plain words — the checks at its top, e.g. *Knows Target (4 s memory) · Health Below (≤ 30%) · Cooldown (8 s)*, or *always* when it has none — and a behavior placed below one that acts *always* is flagged as probably never getting a turn
  - Behaviors that cast from the enemy's **kit** say which pieces they need (*Needs kit: Escape, Grenade*); with an enemy picked, the ones its kit leaves empty are warned about on the behavior, in the library and in the add list, and the enemy's **Kit** can be filled in right there in the Composer
  - Problems the tree checks find inside a behavior (an empty branch, a missing tree, a key nothing writes…) show on its row
  - In Play mode, select an agent: the behaviors it is running right now are highlighted with ▶ (selecting an agent with a different tree brings that tree and enemy up), and each behavior's *When* line marks how its checks last answered for that agent — ✓ held, ✗ didn't, · not asked yet — so "why isn't it pouncing?" is answered at a glance
  - **What if…** (out of Play mode): tick which checks hold — *Knows Target*, *Health Below (≤ 30%)*, … every check the tree's behaviors use is listed — and each list marks with → and a green tint the behavior that would get its turn; untick *Cooldowns, limits and chances are ready* to see what happens while they are not. It reads the checks at the top of each behavior only, so an action failing partway isn't foreseen
- **Behavior settings**: a behavior can name the values worth tuning — in the Behavior Tree Editor select a node and tick, say, its Cooldown's seconds under *Behavior settings* and call it "Pounce Cooldown". Every tree using the behavior then shows it under the row's *Settings* in the Composer and can give it its own value (bold = changed here, ↺ = back to the behavior's own), so a slower pounce for a heavy enemy no longer means copying the behavior. One name on several nodes moves them together. A setting keeps to the node value's own limits (a chance stays within 0–1, a cooldown at 0 s or more), shown next to its name. The library lists each behavior's settings
- **Tune one enemy type without touching the others**: with an enemy picked, the Composer's **Every enemy using this tree / Only <enemy>** switch decides what the on/off boxes and settings change. *Only <enemy>* switches behaviors off and changes settings for that type alone, so several enemy types can share one tree (and get its fixes) yet fight differently — the enemy's value wins over the tree's. Opening the Composer from an enemy whose tree is shared starts in *Only <enemy>*; rows say *(off for <enemy>)*, and settings show what that enemy runs them at. The tree's own settings can be tuned per enemy the same way. The Enemy Data inspector sums up its own tuning, and its checks ignore kit needs of behaviors it switched off and warn about tuning for behaviors its tree doesn't run or settings that no longer exist
- **New Enemy Type** (CGD › AI › New Enemy Type…): pick an existing enemy to start from, give the new one a name, tier and team, choose its behavior tree — share the template's, copy it (all its behaviors, to switch off or reorder), or a starter tree to fill from the library — and tick which other parts it gets its own copy of: senses, target priorities, faction doctrine, voice. Unticked parts stay shared with the template. It makes the new enemy data and a prefab variant of the template (same body and visuals, the new data), and can add it straight to a faction's roster so generated levels spawn it. With a copied or starter tree, it opens in the Behavior Composer
- **Enemy Roster** (CGD › AI › Enemy Roster): every enemy type in one table — tier, team, melee or ranged, toughness (raw damage to kill), melee and ranged damage per second, sight range, run speed, tree and doctrine — sortable by any column and searchable, with a ✓ / ⚠ / ⛔ checks column (hover for the details). Click a name to edit it, a tree to open it
- **Enemy Data inspector**: an *At a glance* box at the top says in plain words what the numbers add up to ("about 18 damage/s, prefers 20 m", "sees 60 m in a 110° cone"), followed by warnings for settings that probably don't do what was meant: no tree (it just stands there), a ranged enemy whose tree never shoots, a preferred range beyond its sight, *Raises Alarm* on with no Raise Alarm node in its tree, a projectile prefab that can't fly, chasing slower than it patrols
- The inspector's **Mind** section lists the assets it thinks with (tree, senses, target priorities, doctrine, voice), how many enemy types share each one, an *Open* button, and **Make Own Copy** — so changing one enemy's tree or temperament never silently changes another's. *Find Prefabs* lists the prefabs using the data; *New Type From This…* starts the New Enemy Type window from it
- A behavior tree's inspector lists the enemy types that think with it and the trees that run it as a behavior, so it's clear whom an edit affects

## Object Pooling
- Frequently spawned objects — projectiles, grenades, zones, pickups, particle effects and enemies — are reused instead of created and destroyed, avoiding hitches and garbage collection
- Pools grow on demand; they can also be pre-filled when a scene starts so the first burst of gunfire or the first enemy wave doesn't stutter
- Reused objects reset themselves (health, AI state, rolled contents), so a recycled object behaves like a fresh one
- Particle effects return themselves to the pool when they finish playing
- Everything still in flight is recalled when a new scene loads, so nothing leaks from one level into the next
- Dead enemies and broken props can be removed after a delay (and reused when they came from a pool)

## Loot & Drops
- Enemies, breakable props and chests drop loot from Loot Table assets
- **Guaranteed drops** — always dropped (a boss key, a quest item)
- **Weighted drops** — a set number of draws (fixed or a range) from a weighted list, with a configurable chance of a draw coming up empty; draws can be made unique so the same entry isn't picked twice
- **Nested tables** — an entry can roll another table, so shared pools like "common ammo" are authored once and reused. The outermost table with rarity checks decides the tier of everything rolled inside it, so a shared pool drops at the odds of the container it was drawn from
- **Rarity** — weapons and gear that drop get a tier (Common → Legendary) by checks from the top down: a chance at Legendary, then at Epic, and so on until Common catches the rest (the default table: Legendary 1%, then Epic 5%, Rare 15%, Uncommon 35%, Common 100% — about 1 / 5 / 14 / 28 / 52% of gear drops). Their stats roll at that tier; a luck value makes empty draws rarer and high tiers likelier
- **Tier pools** — a tier can have its own pool, so landing that tier draws from a different list, all at that tier: a 1% Legendary hit can roll from a Legendary-only table instead of the gear that was drawn
- Drops can be stacks of items, individually rolled weapons and gear, or any world object (health orbs, effects)
- Drops scatter around the source and settle onto the ground; weapons become weapon pickups, everything else an item pickup
- **Searchable furniture** — lockers, storage cabinets, filing cabinets and office desks across the map can each be searched once (hold Interact). Often they are empty; otherwise they hold a draw of resources, ammunition or consumables. Size tells you the odds: small lockers and cabinets hold something about 1 time in 3, ordinary ones (and desks) a little under half the time, large ones nearly 2 times in 3
- **Rooms aren't left blank** — rooms, offices and cubicles with bare floor tend to get a locker or cabinet against a wall, or a desk, so there is something to search. Some small spaces still stay empty
- Breakable props — crates and barrels can be given health so any attack breaks them, spilling their loot
- **Kill credit** — what an enemy or breakable prop drops depends on how much of it you took down yourself: your share of its health, shield and armour-soaked damage, counting only what each hit actually removed (no overkill). Your share scales every drop's chance: take all of it down and you roll the normal odds; take down only 10% before another faction, a room hazard or the enemy itself finishes it, and a 1-in-100 drop becomes 1-in-1000, so letting others do the work saves ammo but costs loot. Burns, poison and bleeds count for whoever applied them. Chests and rewards always give everything
- Included: a default enemy loot table dropping ammo of every caliber and, occasionally, a rolled rifle or SMG
- Loot rolls are seedable: rolling a table from the same seed gives the same drops, and every weapon or piece of gear in them rolls the same stats

## Game Flow
- The game moves between clear states: boot, main menu, loading, playing, paused, level transition, game over and victory; impossible moves (pausing from the main menu, winning while loading) are refused
- Pausing freezes gameplay time and world audio; the settings menu doubles as the pause menu and can't be opened during game over or loading
- Level loading runs in the background with progress for a loading screen, preceded by a short transition for fade-outs or results
- Restart level, return to main menu, start game and quit are available to UI buttons
- The player's death can end the run with a game-over state instead of respawning (per-scene option)
- UI panels can be shown only in certain states (pause panel, game-over screen, loading screen) without code
- The cursor is captured during play and released in menus automatically

## Expeditions (Core Loop)
- A run follows the GDD loop: start in the safe starting room, explore and gather, craft, push to the boss and extract — or die trying
- The starting room is a hub: no enemies spawn there, and a **Hub Workbench** (all crafting recipes) stands beside the spawn spot, so you can come back to craft between pushes
- Every run starts with a freshly rolled **Modular Pistol** (its firing style and stats differ each run) with a randomly rolled Common **Dagger, Sword or Axe** in its offhand, so the first slot is a gun and a melee weapon (the melee key swings it; the pistol keeps its sights; the offhand costs the pistol some accuracy and recoil control, and replaces the pistol's Tactical Knife), and nothing else in the weapon slots, plus random gear — sometimes a Combat Vest — ammunition, bandages, sometimes a combat stim, a frag grenade or two and some scrap metal and cloth. The loadout comes from the level's seed, so a kept seed keeps its loadout
- The Exit room's pad is the **extraction point** (hold Interact on "Extract"): everything you carry — the pack, your weapons and worn armour — goes into the ship's hold
- **Dying loses everything** you carried: what you brought from the ship and what you found. The ship's hold itself is never at risk
- After every run the ship screen shows how it went (EXTRACTED, or KILLED IN ACTION with what was lost), the ship's hold, and the kit for the next run: click an entry to pack it or put it back. **Deploy** starts a fresh run with the packed kit on top of the new starting loadout — armour is worn straight away when its slot is free, weapons fill free slots
- Leaving a level mid-run (restart or main menu) counts as not making it out
- **Run statistics** — the ship screen also shows a line for the run just ended: its length, kills (split by enemy tier 1/2/3), damage dealt and damage taken. Lifetime totals (runs, extractions, deaths, kills, damage, best kills in a run, longest run) are kept between play sessions in their own file; the editor doesn't write them unless *Save In Editor* is ticked on the recorder
- **Ship upgrades** — the ship screen's third column lists permanent upgrades, bought with what extractions bring home: credits plus scrap, cloth or data cores, taken from the ship's hold. Each upgrade has two or three levels bought in order, and every level owned applies on every run from then on — even after a death, so a failed run's earlier haul still counts. The five upgrades: **Hull Plating** (+20/+20/+30 max health), **Reactive Armor** (+10/+15 armour), **Servo Tuning** (+5%/+5% move speed), **Targeting Suite** (+5%/+5%/+10% damage) and **Ability Coolant** (−8%/−8% ability cooldowns). Each entry shows its level, what the next one gives and costs, and is greyed out until the hold can pay for it
- **Saving:** the ship's hold, the kit packed for the next run and the ship's upgrades are saved to disk whenever they change, and are there again the next time the game starts. A run in progress is never saved: quitting mid-run loses what you took in, like leaving the level does. Rolled gear comes back exactly as it was (same stats, name and perks) with its attachments; a loaded magazine doesn't carry over
- A save that can't be read is set aside (kept next to it as `ship.json.unreadable`) and the ship starts empty; items that no longer exist in the game are skipped and the rest loads
- In the editor nothing is saved by default, so every test starts with an empty ship; tick *Save In Editor* on ExpeditionSession to test saving (the editor keeps its own save, apart from a build's)
- An **Emergency Exit** room's escape pod is an early way out (hold Interact on "Emergency extract"): the run ends, your weapons and worn armour make it home, but only half of every stack in your pack fits and loose gear in the pack is left behind. The ship screen reads EMERGENCY EXTRACTION and says how much didn't fit

## Director (Pacing)
An AI director, in the spirit of Left 4 Dead's, paces each play session so fights come in waves of tension and relief instead of a constant grind.
- The director keeps track of how stressed you are: taking damage raises it (a bigger share of your health, a bigger jump), enemies dying close to you raise it a little, and dying fills it. It only eases off once the fighting has stopped for a few seconds
- The session runs in a cycle: **build-up** (pressure grows until you are stressed), a short **peak** held for a few seconds, a **fade** that waits until you have calmed down, then a **calm** of 30–45 seconds with nothing new coming in, before the next build-up
- A level opens with a short calm (about 15 seconds) to look around and get ready
- During build-up and the peak, small groups of 2–4 **reinforcements** arrive every 20–35 seconds, out of your sight and 18–30 m away, and come looking for you. In a generated level they come along the route: during the build-up they arrive in one of the next two rooms on your way to the exit, so the fight is in front of you, and at the peak in one of the last two rooms you came through, so it pushes you on (never in the Start room; anywhere around you when you're in a corridor or no such room will do). At most 6 are around at once; nothing arrives during the fade or the calm. Each arrival is announced in the HUD feed ("Movement nearby"), with a sound from where they came in once one is assigned
- **Adapts to how you play:** coming out of a peak with most of your health makes the next ones harder (bigger groups, sooner, and shorter calms); a peak that nearly killed you, or a death, eases off (smaller groups, later, longer calms). It moves one step per peak and stays within limits, so it never swings wildly
- **Supplies when you need them:** a few seconds into each calm after a peak, if you are below half health with fewer than 2 bandages, or down to under 2 spare magazines for the gun in hand, the director puts down what you need most — 2 bandages, or 3 magazines' worth of that gun's ammo — a few metres away, in sight where possible ("Supplies spotted nearby"). At most one supply per calm, never during the opening calm
- **Adaptive music:** the score is built from layered stems that follow the action — a calm bed, tension creeping in as pressure builds, percussion once the fight is on (dropping out in the calm), and a peak layer only while the peak is held — with stingers marking the peak and the calm after it. It follows the Music volume setting and keeps playing in the pause menu. *No music is assigned yet: the layers and stingers are ready for clips*
- **Enemies follow the pacing**, including the ones placed in the level: the director decides how boldly they fight, never what they know. At the peak they push together — more of them may close in on you or shoot at once, they search longer for you and are harder to shake. In the fade and the calm they hold back: fewer attack at once, searches end sooner and they fall back more readily, so the calm actually feels calm
- **Bold moves are rationed across the level**: flanking, grenades and suppressing fire need the director's go-ahead, and only a few enemies may be making one at once — around four at the peak, two during a build-up and one in the fade or calm (more against a player who is cruising, fewer against one who is struggling). That makes the peak feel like a coordinated assault instead of a grenade storm. Melee enemies don't improvise flanks at all while the director eases off
- **Patrol probes**: during a build-up the director now and then sends a calm enemy some way off to have a look round near you — a rough area, never your exact spot, so it may walk right past. Its "?" shows it's curious, not hunting. At most two per build-up
- **Narration**: the pacing speaks through the HUD feed and the enemies themselves. As the peak begins the nearest enemy that knows you're around shouts to push and the feed warns "They're pushing!"; as things fade and the calm settles in, one calls to hold back ("It's gone quiet..."). Calm patrols never shout, so it doesn't give away enemies that haven't noticed anything
- Without a director in the scene (the AI Simulator scene, the shooting range) enemies fight exactly as tuned
- Respawning starts a fresh calm
- Encounter rooms and the enemies placed in rooms are unaffected; their fights simply count toward your stress like any other

## Map Director (Level Pacing)
Where the pacing director runs a play session, the map director shapes the level itself, before and while it is built, so the fights are paced by the rooms they happen in. It reads the map's difficulty curve and gives every room a part in it:
- **Peak**: the toughest fight of its stretch of map (intense, and more intense than every fight next to it). Two peaks never stand side by side. A peak room is built bigger, an arena, and filled further: more walls, low cover and columns than any other room of its size, so a big fight there has room to move, hide and flank. **Beating it pays**: a reward cache waits at the far end of every peak, so the hardest fight stands between you and the best loot of that stretch
- **Relief**: the fight you walk into straight after a peak. It is a tighter room with fewer enemies (about 60%, always at least one), a breather that is still a fight
- **Build**: the ordinary fights on the way up to the next peak, built as before
- **Rest**: every calm room (Start, Exit, shops, workshops, puzzles, treasure, events, junctions, quiet rooms and the like). Their structure is asked for a little less, so a calm room reads at a glance, and **the pacing director's reinforcements never arrive in them**: a calm room stays calm while you are in it (a group heading your way still comes through the rooms beyond it)
- **Approach**: the fight right before the boss. Lighter on enemies, and an **ammo cache** is always put down in it, against a wall, so you can restock before the boss. Where the room before the boss is already a calm one, it stays the breather
- **Climax**: the boss room, built as a larger arena with more cover
- **Rooms grow as you go deeper**: fight rooms near the Start are a little smaller, and they grow toward the boss (about 10% either way), so the early fights are tight and the late ones open into arenas. Calm rooms keep their size
- **No room twice in a row**: a room is very rarely the same place as a room it opens onto (two bars or two gyms side by side), so the walk from room to room keeps changing (about 1 link in 100 instead of 4)
- **Cover never closes the way round**: when a room is built up with extra walls and cover, an addition that would leave a fight room with only one lane from its way in to its far end is turned down, in every room, directed or not. More rooms keep a way round than before (about 7 in 10 fights instead of 6)
- **Furniture never closes it either**: a fight room's main lane and the way round it are kept clear when it is furnished (rugs and other things you walk over still go there), so the way round the walls left survives the crates and shelves. About 2 in 3 furnished fight rooms now have one, where furniture used to close all but 1 in 3, with almost as much furniture as before
- **Peaks take the big rooms**: when a peak ends up beside a clearly bigger ordinary fight (a hall spanning several cells), the peak moves into the hall and the relief follows it, so the set-piece fight happens in the biggest room around instead of the one next to it
- Fights with waves and no enemies placed in advance (Holdout, Ambush, Rift, Siege, Gauntlet, Spawner, Haunt) count as fights
- **The way to the boss is paced like a sawtooth**: more than 3 ordinary fights in a row on the main path get the strongest of them made a peak (with the relief after it), and a way to the boss with no peak at all gets its strongest fight made one, so every run has a high point before the boss and is never a long flat slog
- **The director checks its own work**: once a level is built, a peak or boss room that came out with no more cover for its size than an ordinary fight next to it is built again (up to 3 more times) and the best one kept, one that keeps a way round first. What it can't put right is noted: a peak smaller than a fight beside it, a relief no smaller than its peak, supplies that found no place, more than 3 calm rooms in a row on the way to the boss, or the strongest peak early with only weaker ones after it
- In the Scene view's room overview each room's label names its part ("Peak beat", "Relief beat", ...), and what the director changed ("Director: flat stretch, fixed") or found wrong ("! under-covered") in it
- **Built-level sweep** (editor): the Map Style Report window (the Map Graph window's *Analyze*) can build a style's levels for many seeds and show, per part, the rooms' size, cover, how far they see, enemies and how many keep a way round, how often each check was fixed or left, and the seeds that left the most to look at
- The same seed gives the same parts and the same rooms. Without a map director in the build settings every room is built alike, as before

## Encounter Rooms
Rooms with rules of their own, mixed into generated maps alongside the usual fights, shops and treasure. They are the exception: in normal rooms (Combat, Breach and the rest) the doors never lock and the enemies simply patrol. Each one says what it is on its door sign (except the Ambush), and the map's validity checks still hold for all of them.
- Encounter rewards (Lockdown, Holdout, Ambush, Stealth vault, Rift, Puzzle) are the same chest as a Treasure room's: anything and everything, at the room's luck
- **Lockdown** — step inside and blast shutters slam down over every doorway. They only open once every enemy in the room is dead, and a reward chest drops. Enemies that wandered out before the doors closed are shut outside and don't count; an empty room calls in a wave instead. The doors can't stay down for good: an enemy that can't be reached (outside the room, off the floor, or with no way to walk to it) is let go of after 20 seconds and stops counting, though it stays alive, and if nothing dies for 150 seconds the room lets go of whoever is left, says so, and opens. Ambush and Rift rooms get the same safety net on their waves
- **Holdout** — an uplink terminal. Starting it (hold Interact) seals the room and enemies pour in every ~18 seconds while it uploads (75 s, with a countdown). The uplink sends out a signal every few seconds that hostile enemies in earshot (30 m) go to investigate, so they head for the terminal instead of waiting to be found. When it finishes the shutters open, a reward drops, and the whole ship map is downloaded to your world map
- **Puzzle** — the doors leading deeper are locked blue bulkheads until the room's calibration ring is solved: 4–5 consoles, each flipping itself and its two neighbours (the prompt says which), and every light must be green. The way back stays open, so you can always leave and return. A puzzle room with nowhere deeper to go drops a reward instead
- **Ambush** — looks exactly like a Treasure room, down to the door sign and the chest in the middle. Open the chest and the doors seal while two waves drop in. The loot is real
- **Stealth** — a guarded vault room, with one or two see-through concealment patches to slip between, which hide you from every guard standing outside them, however close (as in the AI Simulator arena). While no guard has spotted you, the vault holds extra loot. The first guard to give chase raises the alarm: the room seals, the bonus is gone, and the guards plus reinforcements must be beaten before the shutters open
- **Rift** — a tear between the room's two realities around a glowing core. The room seals and waves come through from one reality, then the other, the last from both at once; when the last falls the rift collapses and a reward drops
- **Gamble** — two machines: one takes credits, the other takes blood (a share of your health, never enough to kill you). Each pull busts, wins a normal drop or hits the jackpot (a drop with much better luck); every pull costs more than the last, and each machine runs dry after three
- **Spawner** — a structure in the middle of the room (a red pillar) keeps sending enemies of the room's faction while you are near, a few at a time and never more than a handful alive at once; the doors stay open, so you can fight through or walk away. Destroying it stops the flow and drops a reward. The enemies it sends drop nothing, so it can't be farmed: the structure is the payout
- **Workshop** — a calm stop with a crafting bench in the middle of the room, for building and upgrading mid-run
- **Shrine** — a few altars, each offering a different boon (a blessing, sometimes with a curse) that lasts for the rest of the level. Take one and the others close; no enemies
- **Siege** — an objective stands in the middle of the room. Stepping in seals the doors and waves come for the objective (and you) until a timer runs out; hold it and the doors open with a reward. If the objective falls first, the doors open and there is no reward
- **Gauntlet** — the doors seal and waves come one after another, each bigger than the last, each laying a random twist on you (a boon or curse that lasts for that wave). Clear them all for a reward that gets luckier with every wave survived
- **Haunt** — a Void entity stalks the room, and it can't be killed: bring it down and it is only banished, coming back about 25 seconds later somewhere away from you. Three anchors (violet pillars) stand round the room and bind it there; break them all and it comes undone for good and a reward drops. The doors stay open, so you can fight it off while you work, slip past it, or leave it be. The entity drops nothing itself. While you are inside, the objective panel shows the anchors broken, the entity's health, and how long until it returns. The other factions treat it as Void: Bio flees it, Tech fights it. A Haunt can turn up in any faction's territory — it is where Void bleeds through
- **Boss** — the last room before the Exit. Stepping inside brings out the boss — a much tougher, larger version of one of the room's strongest enemies, by default eight times the health with extra armour — and seals every doorway, including the one to the Exit: **extraction only opens once the boss is dead**. It comes straight for you. As its health drops past two thirds and one third it escalates: it hits harder, moves and attacks faster, and calls in reinforcements from the room's faction, each step announced. A big enough hit past both thresholds triggers both at once, and healing never undoes a phase. When it falls the room opens, it drops its own loot, and two reward chests with extra luck appear. Each faction can have a boss of its own
- **Objective panel** — while a Siege or Gauntlet runs, a panel at the top of the screen shows it: Siege has a countdown, the wave count and the objective's health bar; Gauntlet has the wave count and the current twist; a boss fight shows the boss's name, health bar and phase (Phase 2/3)
- **Hazard** — a leak (fire, toxin, coolant or live wiring) that keeps afflicting everyone on the room's floor — you and the enemies alike — until you find and switch on the vent controls somewhere in the room
- **Emergency Exit** — an escape pod off the main path (see Expeditions)
- **Junction** — a small crossroads where three or more routes meet: no enemies, no encounter, just a place to pick a way. A map holds at most a few, never next to each other, and only where a room already has three or more doors
- **Quiet** — once in a while (at most two per map, never next to each other) a room is simply peaceful: no enemies at all, and always something worth stopping for — a salvage node (scrap metal), an ammo cache or a loot cache
- Lockdown, Holdout and Rift rooms carry the red danger marker on their door signs; a sealed room announces itself, and every wave and countdown is called out in the HUD feed

## Map Objectives
- Every generated level hands out **main objectives** (1–2) and **side objectives** (0–3), rolled from objective templates with the level's seed — the same seed always gives the same objectives in the same rooms
- An objective is 1–3 steps, each in a specific room of the map: **Clear** it (kill every enemy there), **Activate** a console placed in it, or **Retrieve** a Data Core placed in it. Steps can go in order (the next one appears when the last is done) or all at once
- Each step asks for a **range** of room tiers, so every map rolls its own combination — a three-step objective can come out 1-1-3, 1-2-2, 2-2-3, 3-1-2… A template can also say how the tiers relate: **any**, **never going down** (each step at least as tough as the last), or **all the same tier** (1-1-1 or 3-3-3). Fixed shapes work too: a strict climb 1 → 2 → 3, all in tier-1 rooms, all in tier-3 rooms, or all in rooms of **one reality**
- **Main objectives** lock the Boss room: its doors show "Objectives 0/2" and open once every main objective is done
- **Side objectives** are optional and pay out when done: credits (25 / 50 / 100 by the highest room tier involved) and a reward cache in the last step's room, with better odds the higher the tier — a tier-3 side objective is the risky, rich one
- Objectives show in the quest tracker ("Main: …", "Side: …") and each active step's room is marked on the world map and minimap (even unexplored, pinned to the edge when out of view)
- Only rooms reachable before the Boss are used (no secret rooms, Start, Exit or Shop), no room hosts two steps, and objectives that don't fit a map are skipped. Rooms already cleared before a Clear step unlocks count straight away
- Included templates — main: Escalation (clear tier 1 → 2 → 3), Override Sequence (three consoles anywhere, any order), Secure the Route (clear a tier 2 then a tier 3); either: Data Trail (console tier 1 → data core tier 2 → console tier 3); Mixed Ops (clear, console, data core, each no easier than the last — 1-1-3, 1-2-2…); side: Salvage Run (two data cores in tier-1 rooms), Deep Recovery (a data core in a tier-3 room), Purge (two tier-3 rooms of one reality), Faction Sweep (three rooms of one reality), Supply Line (two data cores in tier 1–2 rooms, then a console in a tougher tier 2–3 room), Wildcard (three rooms of one tier, whichever the map rolls)

## Targeting
- One shared set of targeting rules for abilities, attacks and effects: **Self**, **Raycast** (under the crosshair), **Area** (around the caster or where they aim on the ground), **Cone**, **Nearest** (the N closest), and **Ground** (a point, no characters)
- Each rule picks by relation — self, allies, enemies, neutral, or any mix — and can require clear line of sight and cap the number of targets (nearest first)
- Targeting rules are assets, so a new ability can reuse "enemies in a 60° cone" or "allies within 8 m" without code
- Enemy AI uses the same system to find the nearest visible hostile

## Resources (Meters)
- A generic system for consumable, regenerating values: stamina, mana, energy, rage, oxygen, battery charge, and so on — each defined by an asset with a name, colour, capacity, starting fill and regeneration
- Regeneration can refill (stamina, mana) or decay (rage), and pauses for a moment after the meter is pushed the other way
- Exhaustion — a meter emptied to zero can be locked until it refills past a threshold
- Meters can be spent all-or-nothing (ability costs), drained continuously (sprinting), restored, and have their capacity changed by upgrades
- Resource zones drain or restore a meter while you stand in them — water drains oxygen, a shrine restores mana — and can hurt you once the meter is empty (drowning)
- A HUD panel shows a coloured bar for each of the player's meters
- Shields run on the same regeneration model
- Included meters: Stamina, Mana, Oxygen and Rage

## Audio System
- All audio plays through a pooled AudioSource system — no per-shot Instantiate/Destroy, sources are reused automatically
- Each SoundBank can route to an Audio Mixer group so categories (SFX, footsteps, ...) can be balanced separately
- Sound data is driven by SoundBank ScriptableObject assets: assign multiple AudioClip variants with pitch and volume randomization so repeated sounds (gunfire, footsteps) never sound robotic
- Weapon audio: each WeaponData asset has optional SoundBank fields for fire, reload, and empty-magazine click; sounds play automatically at the muzzle position
- Melee audio: each MeleeAttackStep has optional swing and hit SoundBanks; swing plays on attack start, hit plays only when a target is struck
- Grenade audio: throw sound on release, explosion sound on detonation (plays via the pool so it persists after the grenade object is destroyed)
- Enemy audio: EnemyData has an optional attack SoundBank played on each melee strike; an EnemyAudio component plays hurt and death sounds
- Player feedback audio: a PlayerAudio component subscribes to the player's health events and plays hurt/death sounds
- Surface-aware footsteps: PlayerFootsteps raycasts downward each step to identify the ground surface; a SurfaceDatabase ScriptableObject maps PhysicMaterials to separate walk, sprint, and crouch SoundBanks; individual surfaces can override via a SurfaceTag component; step interval scales with movement speed
- All sound fields are optional — systems work silently when no SoundBank is assigned, same as before
- Each SoundBank follows one of the settings menu's volume sliders (Effects, Music or Interface), on top of Master
- Mixer support (optional): the volume sliders drive an Audio Mixer, so sounds can be shaped as a whole. The sound dips and muffles while the game is paused, when health is low and when the player is stunned, fading in and out smoothly; the strongest situation wins when several overlap. Without a mixer the sliders work exactly as before

## Death & Respawn
- When health reaches zero the player loses control, the HUD hides, and a death screen is shown. A dodge or ledge climb in progress stops (its invulnerability with it), and a player killed in mid-air falls to the ground rather than hanging there
- Player automatically respawns after a short delay, returning to the designated spawn point
- On respawn, health is restored and every carried weapon's magazine is refilled; reserve ammo in the shared inventory pool is not touched. Where a scene ends the run on death instead (the generated levels), everything carried is lost — see Expeditions. Ability cooldowns reset, and leftover momentum and status effects are cleared

## Map Graph Generation
- Maps start as a pure experience graph — rooms and connections without geometry; a [generated level](#generated-levels) turns one into a playable level. Each node is what the player meets there (Start, Combat, Puzzle, Shop, Event, Treasure, Resupply, Breach, Boss, Exit, and the [encounter rooms](#encounter-rooms) Lockdown, Holdout, Ambush, Stealth, Rift, Gamble, Emergency Exit, Hazard, Spawner, Shrine, Siege, Gauntlet and Haunt, plus the Workshop rest stop, the occasional peaceful Quiet room and small Junction crossroads), and connections say how they link: normal, shortcut, secret or locked — and any open passage can be one-way
- One generator covers many styles of map: a near-straight corridor, a branching spine, a hub around the Start room, a winding labyrinth, or anything in between
- A map style is made of two independent parts that mix freely: a **layout** (the map's shape) and **content** (what fills it). The same Treasure Hunt content works on a hub or a labyrinth, and the same labyrinth can be a treasure hunt or a gauntlet
- A style can list several layouts with weights, so its shape changes from run to run (a hub one time, a labyrinth the next) while what fills it stays the same
- Every map has a main path from Start to the Boss and Exit, which can run straight or wind. On top of the main path, each layout adds a set number of optional rooms off it: short side rooms or long wings, straight or twisting, some forking off other branches into deeper areas, some starting from the Start room to make it a hub, and some looping back into a neighbouring room instead of dead-ending. Every map gets its optional rooms however long its main path turns out
- Loops link neighbouring rooms into a web, and shortcuts let the player jump from a shallow room to a deeper one nearby. The Boss always keeps a single entrance and is never closer to Start than the set minimum, whichever route is taken
- How compact a map is can be limited: tightly packed maps tangle into more loops, unbounded ones sprawl
- Optional areas with only one way in can get a locked or secret entrance; since there's no other way in, a gate can't be walked around. Gates can be kept away from the start of the map, so the first few rooms are always open
- **Main locks**: every layout also puts locked doors on the main path itself (Linear 1, Labyrinth 2, the rest 1–2), so the way to the Boss stays shut until their keycard or terminals are found in the side rooms before them. Nothing leads around a main lock — any passage that would is left out of the map, and loops and shortcuts never cross one — and each key sits past the previous main lock, so the run moves forward one locked section at a time. The door into the Boss room is never one — the main objectives already lock it
- Every locked door has a key, placed in a room the player can reach without opening any door or finding any secret (other than the main locks that come before it), so a map can never lock the player out. Keys sit no deeper into the map than the door they open, and prefer side rooms off the main path so getting into a locked area means exploring first
- Locks come in two kinds: a **keycard** (one key in one room) or **terminals** (2–3 terminals, each in a different room, all of which must be switched on). Terminals follow the same rules as keys, so they're always reachable before their door, and no two locks share a room. How often a lock uses terminals, and how many, is set per layout
- Guaranteed rooms are a list, each a room type, where it goes and how many: a reward in every locked or secret area, at its far end, so finding the key or the hidden wall always pays off (a Treasure by default); a room right before the Boss as a last stop (for example a Shop); or a number of rooms within a depth band from Start (for example an early Treasure 3–4 rooms in, or two Events within the first four rooms)
- **Room tiers**: every room is tier 1, 2 or 3 by how deep it lies between Start and the Boss, with a little randomness — rooms near Start are tier 1, the middle of the map turns tier 2 and the stretch before the Boss is mostly tier 3, so the map gets harder as you push on. Depth is a guideline, not a rule: now and then (15%) a room lands a tier above or below what its depth suggests, so a tough room can turn up early or a calm one late. Rooms right next to Start are always tier 1, so every map eases in (Start, Exit, Boss and Shop rooms stay tier 1; harder-run modifiers push tiers up). A room's tier decides how often its enemies are tier 2 or 3, gives its chests, caches and encounter rewards slightly better odds (+0.5 / +1 loot luck at tier 2 / 3), and makes its resource nodes yield more — and a rarer resource where one is set. Tier 3 replaces the old Elite rooms; a hand-made map that still has an Elite room treats it as a tier-3 fight. Tiers show in the map editor and can be set by hand
- Pacing along the main path: never more than a set number of fights in a row (3 by default), whatever their tier — tier-3 fights can come back to back. Every room with a fight in it counts, encounters too (Ambush, Stealth, Hazard, Spawner, Siege, Gauntlet, Haunt as well as Combat, Breach, Lockdown, Holdout and Rift), the same rooms the map director treats as fights, so the map is generated with the rhythm the director reads from it. When no calm room type fits, a rest room (an Event by default) is used, and the rare case where that goes over its limit is reported
- Room types can keep their distance: a minimum number of connections between two rooms of the same type (Treasure rooms are at least three apart); a minimum number of rooms from Start (Treasure is never closer than three rooms, so the first reward is earned); and types that want space (Combat) prefer rooms with open space beside them, so big arenas like cargo bays can stretch out
- Resupply rooms: calm stops on the main path, midway through the run, with an ammo cache
- Breach rooms: fights where two realities overlap — the room belongs to one faction, a neighbouring one bleeds in, and enemies from both rosters fill it. They count as fights for pacing and come no earlier than a third of the way in
- **Who fights whom**: Bio, Tech and Void are all hostile to one another and to you, in generated levels as well as the AI Simulator arena. A room held by two factions (a Breach or Rift room) pits them against each other as well as you, and enemies of different factions that meet anywhere fight
  - **Calm truce**: in generated levels rival factions leave each other alone while calm, so a Breach room is still waiting for you when you get there. Once something starts a fight — they notice you, hear gunfire, or get hit — they turn on each other too. Make noise in a room held by two factions and they may do your work for you
  - **Food chain**: each faction treats the others its own way. **Bio** hunts Tech (it goes for a machine before anything else it can see) and flees Void: sensing Void in a fight wears its nerve down until it breaks and runs. Out of a fight, a Void nearby no longer keeps it from steadying. **Void** hunts Bio and avoids Tech, never starting a fight with it. **Tech** fights everything it meets. A faction that avoids or flees another still fights back when touched or hurt by it. In the AI Simulator the food chain is off for the fighting factions: Bio, Tech and Void all attack each other on sight (Bio still prefers Tech and Void still prefers Bio as targets, and Prey is still hunted by Bio)
  - **Cleaner Bot** (TECH, Service tier): a small flat disc with a waist light and a stub antenna, built to keep floors clean, not to fight. It roams like a roomba: steady pace, keeping to walls and edges, steering to ground it has not covered lately (what it has covered gets dirty again after a minute), stopping to sweep, spot-clean or pause, and going over to dust consoles, desks, kiosks, screens, capacitor banks, breaker panels, cabinets and lab benches for a few seconds each. It is frail (25 health, 20 shield, 20 armour) and has no real attack. It is also a lookout: on seeing a hostile it backs away to about 14 m, keeps watching, and radios every Tech agent in range, through walls, where the threat is and keeps passing on where it moves while it can see it, and again every few seconds; it also reports fallen kin and goes to look into noises. If a hostile gets within 7 m it backs off, and when it loses sight it clears out
  - **Wildlife in generated levels**: BIO-held calm rooms (Combat, Quiet, Junction, Resupply, Workshop, Treasure, Event) hold a group of 1–3 Grazers 40% of the time. TECH-held calm rooms hold one or two Cleaner Bots half the time. Neither are part of the room's fight or enemy count and never seal or count toward clearing a room, they stay in their room's territory, and Bio enemies of the level hunt them. The same seed places the same animals
  - **Prey**: grazers (the *Prey* side) belong to no faction. Bio hunts them (always, calm or not, as it hunts Tech), Tech ignores them and Void treats them like any other hostile; they run from all of them and from you. Their drops stay where they fell whoever made the kill. Like Cleaner Bots they are outside the director's pacing: hurting or killing one adds no stress and is no combat, and the director never picks one to probe or speak
  - **Bio feeds**: after a fight it wins, a Bio walks to what it killed (any other faction's dead, never its own or you) and feeds on it for a few seconds instead of standing over it. It heals while it feeds, and with its head down it is far slower to notice you — a window to slip past or strike first. Leave a pack to feast and it comes back healthier
  - The director's reinforcements arrive one faction to a group
- **The dead stay dead**: enemies placed in a room when the level is built never come back once killed, so a cleared room stays cleared. New enemies come only from the director's reinforcements (which arrive during build-ups, away from you, and come looking) and from encounters (waves, spawners, a boss's calls for help)
- One-way shortcuts: some shortcuts are barred from the near side and can only be opened from their deep end, Dark Souls style. They're no way ahead, but once found from the far side they're a permanent quick route back toward Start — after a death, straight back to where you were
- Ship sections: the run passes through Habitation, then Commerce, then Engineering, from Start to the Boss. Each room belongs to the section of its stretch of the run, so quarters and lobbies cluster in Habitation, restaurants, casinos and parks in Commerce, and cargo bays, docking bays, the reactor and supply depots in Engineering
- Room categories: every room is a kind of ship space (Residential, Recreation, Entertainment, Commercial, Medical, Security, Engineering, Command, Agriculture, Science, Transit, Worship), and rooms sit in the ship's three zones — Civilian, Operations and Services. The generator splits the map into zone areas first, then picks each room's category from its zone. How well categories suit their neighbours decides the pick: casinos like restaurants, engineering doesn't like cabins, and neighbours that touch through a direct door count double. Every rule is a preference, so an odd neighbour still turns up now and then. A category is never tied to a node type: any room — the start, a shop, the boss — can be any kind of space. Categories can be set by hand in the map editor; a locked room keeps its category when the map is regenerated, the validator flags clashing neighbours, and a room's place comes from its category alone, never its node type (a Recreation room is a park or a pool, whether it holds a fight, a shop or the boss); a hand-built landmark appears at most once per map. All twelve are switched on, each with a few general places, one for every class of passenger rather than a version per class: quarters, cabin rows, commons and schools (Residential); parks, pools, gyms, wellness centers and observation decks (Recreation); casinos, theatres, bars and lounges, exhibit halls and the grand atrium (Entertainment); the promenade, restaurants, food courts and kitchens (Commercial); medical bays, pharmacies, wards and morgues (Medical); security offices, armories and barracks (Security); workshops, processing plants, life support, engine rooms and the reactor core (Engineering); the bridge, control rooms and offices (Command); hydroponics bays, farms and water treatment (Agriculture); laboratories, data centers and libraries (Science); lobbies, cargo and docking bays, hangars, escape pod bays, storage and junctions (Transit); and chapels and shrines (Worship). Big halls (hangar, theatre, engine room, grand atrium) turn up less often, and some places are one of a kind — a map has at most one bridge and one grand atrium. The new places are plain placeholder rooms for now. The map editor can colour rooms by category or zone, and categories can be rerolled on their own
- Run modifiers come in two kinds. **Warnings** make a run harder and pay for it with luckier loot in every chest and cache of the level: Lockdown (more locked doors and gates), Infestation (one faction overruns most of the ship, a little tougher) and Overrun (more fights, tougher overall and one extra tier-3 room). **Anomalies** are twists that change how a run plays without making it harder: Scavenger (more Treasure, fewer Shops) and Fractured Hull (extra rooms, more loops and more secret passages). A map gets at most two warnings and one anomaly. They're announced when the level starts (warnings in warning colours), and a modifier is part of the seed, so the same seed always gets the same twist
- **Two ways to grow a map** (per layout): **Path First** walks a main path from Start to the Boss and hangs branches and a few loops off it — tight control over path length and pacing. **Organic** spreads rooms out from Start one at a time, linking each to the room it grew from and, by chance, to the other rooms it lands beside, so the map grows as a web of crossings with no visible spine; the Boss is placed last, beside the farthest room from Start (at least Min Boss Depth away), with the Exit behind it. Organic layouts are tuned by Intersection Chance (how often rooms that meet link up) and Compactness (sprawling arms or a dense block). The ready-made `OrganicMapGenerationSettings` style uses it, and the Random style now picks Organic now and then too
- **Path direction** (per layout): East maps read left to right like a timeline, with the Boss always east of Start. Any-direction maps send the main path off north, south, east or west, each step further from Start, so Start can sit in the middle with branches on every side and the Boss in any direction. All ready-made layouts use East for now
- **Direct doors**: rooms open straight into each other as standard. In every ready-made layout each link between neighbouring rooms, locked, secret and one-way ones included, is a direct door: the two rooms sit side by side with one shared doorway (which carries the gate) and no hallway between them. Where two rooms' shapes or the rooms around them won't let them touch, they are pulled as close as they can be and joined by a short straight passage. Longer, winding hallways are left for links between rooms further apart, and the rare pair no straight passage can join (it takes the shortest route there is, and the build warnings say so). Rooms already joined move together as a block to meet the next room, so whole wings of rooms open straight into each other. A direct door can be toggled per connection in the map editor (shown as "Direct" on the link). The room generator scene's neighbouring rooms open straight into its room the same way
- Ready-made layouts: Linear, Branching, Hub, Labyrinth and Balanced (in between Hub and Labyrinth: a moderately winding path, one or two branches off Start, a handful of loops). Ready-made content: Standard (a balanced mix), Treasure Hunt (more Treasure and Puzzle rooms, an early Treasure, a gentler difficulty curve) and Gauntlet (mostly fights, little loot, a Shop right before the Boss, a steeper difficulty curve). All of them use the TECH, BIO and VOID factions (Faction assets with a name, colour and enemy roster)
- Ready-made styles: `LinearMapGenerationSettings`, `BranchingMapGenerationSettings`, `HubMapGenerationSettings`, `LabyrinthMapGenerationSettings` and `BalancedMapGenerationSettings` (each layout with Standard content), and `RandomMapGenerationSettings`, which picks Branching or Hub most often and Labyrinth or Linear now and then. Each run has a chance of one run modifier
- The same seed and settings always give the same map, so a good map can be kept by its seed
- A map seed can be a number or any text ("banana", "run 2"), typed in the Map Graph editor or on the level; text seeds are shown as typed. Maps saved with number seeds stay exactly the same
- Each generation layer — run modifiers, layout, room types, intensity, factions — can be rerolled on its own from the editor: reroll the room types and the layout stays exactly the same; reroll factions and types and intensity stay the same. Rerolling the layout can also switch a multi-layout style to another of its shapes. A new seed starts every layer fresh
- A layout sets the number of optional rooms, minimum boss depth, how far the map may spread, a connection limit per room, main path length and winding, branch length, winding, fork, hub and rejoin chances, how many loops and shortcuts (and how far a shortcut reaches and skips), gate chances, how many gates and how deep the first may be, and where keys go
- Content sets, for each room type, a minimum and maximum count, a weight, where it can go (anywhere, main path only, branches only), how deep into the run it can appear, whether two of the same type can sit next to each other, how far apart two of them must be, whether it wants space around it, and whether it prefers dead ends (for example, treasure as a reward for exploring); plus the guaranteed rooms, the pacing, the ship sections, the difficulty curve and the factions
- Room types with the tightest placement rules are placed first, so broad types like Combat can't take the only rooms a Treasure could use. Guaranteed rooms count toward their type's limits. Constraints that can't be met are reported instead of failing silently
- Each room gets an intensity from a difficulty curve over the run (rising, a breather before the boss, then the boss at full intensity), adjusted per room type with a little variation
- The colony ship has been warped by an anomaly into three realities — the TECH, BIO and VOID factions — and it splits the ship between them differently every run. Each map rolls a faction mix:
  - **Infested** — one faction, different each time, holds nearly the whole ship, with the others clinging to a room or two
  - **Balanced** — the factions split the ship evenly into territories of their own
  - **Contested** — uneven territories broken into many small pockets, so the realities interleave across the ship
  - **Warped** — no territories at all: every room is a random faction
- Even in a territory, the anomaly flips the odd room to a random faction, so a run can always surprise. Territories grow out from rooms spread across the map, and a faction cut off by another's rooms takes the nearest free rooms past them as enclaves
- Content decides how often each mix comes up (Gauntlet leans Infested, Treasure Hunt leans Warped), and the Infestation modifier always makes a map Infested
- Every room is always one of the three realities — there are no neutral rooms. A faction sets how a room looks and whose enemies appear there, never what the room is: a Treasure room, a Resupply stop or a peaceful Event in BIO territory is still loot, ammo or calm, just in BIO's style. In a generated level a room's enemies come from its faction's roster (how many still depends on the room type, so peaceful rooms stay empty) and its floor takes on the faction's colour, so territory is visible in the world and on the captured map

Maps fill in a few at a time, so the window stays responsive

The report can be copied as text. The first seed can be a number or any text, and the rest count up from it so each map's seed can be typed back into the editor: `100` gives 100, 101…; `run 7` gives run 7, run 8…; `banana` gives banana 1, banana 2…
- A saved map keeps the run modifiers and faction mix it was generated with, so changing a style's lists later never quietly changes a hand-tuned map; regenerating rolls them afresh
- Clicking a validation issue that names a room or connection selects it and centres the view on it
- Every room shows its faction as a small chip in the faction's colour (TECH, BIO, VOID) on its corner, in every view mode — Breach rooms show both factions; locked doors are numbered on the graph (L1, L2…) and labelled "Main lock" when they stand between Start and the Exit, with their key room or terminal rooms; each room holding a key or terminal wears a matching badge ("L1 key", "L2 T1"), and with objectives shown the Boss wears a badge naming the main objectives that open it ("needs A B"); the side panel shows the run's modifiers and faction mix, which layout the map was built on (and its room range), whether a room sits behind a gate, and which door's key or terminal it holds
- **Blueprint** mode (Map Graph window) shows the graph as the level it becomes: a top-down floor plan laid out exactly as a generated level would be — room shapes and sizes, multi-cell halls, corridors, walls and inner walls, pillars and landmark furniture, and coloured markers on locked (yellow), secret (purple) and one-way (cyan) doorways. Rooms are filled by room type or faction and labelled with their place on the ship. It updates as the graph is edited; clicking a room selects its node, so it can be edited from the side panel while watching the plan change. Layout problems (such as crossing corridors) show along the bottom

New seeds of that style grow different maps that feel like yours. Types you never used are switched off; with fewer than three examples the ranges are widened a little so new maps don't come out as clones
- **Objectives on the graph**: the Map Graph window shows the objectives a level built from the graph would get — the same plan, from the same seed. - **Grid snapping**: with Snap on (the default), the canvas shows the room grid the level is built on — one cell per room — and a moved or added node drops into the nearest free cell, with an outline showing where it will land. What the graph shows is then exactly how the rooms are laid out in 3D. Right-click the canvas → Snap All Nodes to Grid tidies a graph drawn without it
- **Pinned places**: a room's Place field (side panel) pins exactly which place the level builds there — this room is the casino, that one the bridge — instead of letting the level pick one of the category's. Pinning a place sets the room's category to match, and "Pin current" keeps the place the room gets now, so a place you like survives a new seed. A place with several layouts also gets a Layout choice under it, to pin which arrangement is built (a casino's table pits rather than its slot hall); it is kept with the place when the map is regenerated. A locked room keeps its pinned place when the map is regenerated, a one-of-a-kind place pinned somewhere is never handed to another room, and the validator flags a pinned place that doesn't match the room's category or a one-of-a-kind place pinned twice. Pinned places read "(pinned)" on the room card and in the hover panel
- **Room cards** (Details toggle, on by default): besides its colour, every room shows its type, id and edit mark; its tier as a coloured T1/T2/T3 chip; its intensity as a bar along the bottom; the place the level will give it and how many cells it spans ("Restaurant 2×1 · Commercial"); its depth and route (main path, bypassable, required or which branch; dead end; behind a gate); how many enemies it starts with, its links against the connection limit and its ship section; and a red "!n" chip when validation issues name it or one of its links. When colour shows something other than room type, a stripe down the left edge keeps the type visible. Lines drop away as you zoom out. - View modes colour the graph by room type, intensity, faction (each in its reality's colour: TECH amber, BIO green, VOID violet), ship section, room category, required vs optional (rooms every route to the Exit must pass through), or branch. - **Room Lab** (Window › CGD › Room Lab, *Open in Room Lab* on any room function, *Room Lab* on a node in the Map Graph side panel, or double-click a room in Blueprint mode): builds one room on its own, exactly as a level builds it — same floor plans, structure and furniture from the same seeds — so a room function, dressing or faction design can be tuned without generating and playing a whole map
  - **Made-up rooms**: pick a room type, category, place (only the places the build settings could actually pick are listed; *pick* lets the generator choose as it would), its layout (for places with several; *pick* chooses by weight as a level does — *Open in Room Lab* on a layout asset opens it directly), faction, tier, intensity, the floor levels it may be sunk and raised to (as the RoomGenerator scene's Inspector; 0 and 0 builds it flat) and which walls get a doorway, then step through seeds. **Map rooms**: pick a saved map graph and one of its rooms to see exactly that room, neighbours and corridors included; *Try variations of this room* copies it into a made-up room to experiment with
  - The plan shows the room's walls, pillars, inner walls, doorways and furniture, with one generation pass painted over it at a time: **Tags** (walkways, doorway fronts, kept-clear access floor, the centre spot, corners and wall edges), **Zones** (areas inner walls split the room into) or **Sightlines** (how far you can see along each tile's row and column, green to red). Hovering a tile says what it is, how far it sees and what stands on it and which way it faces
  - The report sums the room up (place, size and height, floor, doorways, pillars and inner walls, how much floor the furniture takes) and checks it: doorways that didn't fit, floor that can't be walked to, and every furnishing that came up short — with why (the room's share of floor for props was used up, no tile matched its rules, or a required piece needed its rules loosened). Clicking a check outlines the tiles it is about. Every furnishing is listed in placement order with how many were placed out of how many it asked for
  - **A layout for every place**: each of the 48 places is built to its layout pattern. Halls get a pillar grid; aisle rooms (library, storage, data center, armory, hydroponics) tall shelving rows, and theatres, chapels, schools, control rooms, wards, barracks, gyms and processing plants low rows of seats, pews or benches to shoot over — rows break every few tiles so the aisles can be crossed and never block a doorway or route. Offices and cabin rows are mazes of small rooms; medical bays, labs and spas are split by a divider; security offices have a checkpoint; pools, bridges and shrines a ring of columns; bars and pharmacies a counter; machine rooms columns set into their corners. Parks, farms and junctions take the holding faction's layout
  - **Realities still show**: on top of a place's own layout, BIO adds ruined wall stubs and VOID full-height folds that pinch the floor into corridors; TECH adds nothing, keeping its lanes open. A place without a layout of its own is built in the faction's style (BIO broken growth columns, VOID an office maze, TECH a grid of pylons with a checkpoint)
  - **Room composition**: the big open places are laid out on classic map patterns, so their floor plan alone says how they play:
    - **Three lanes**: two long walls run down the room, splitting it into a wide middle lane and two side lanes, joined at both ends and through a gap or two partway along. The middle is the direct, exposed way through; the side lanes are the way round. Galleries, loading docks, assembly bays and sorting floors have high walls that hide the side lanes; boulevards, galleria atriums, open-plan offices and grow halls have waist-high planter rails and partitions you can shoot over.
    - **Hub**: a waist-high rail walls a square court round the room's centrepiece, with a gap in the middle of each side and a loop of open floor round it, so fights circle the court. Fountain courts, sculpture courts, reception halls and plaza gardens.
    - **Pavilions**: small walled structures stand in the open floor of big halls: a restroom block, a bar hut or a kiosk in guest rooms, and a store shed, guard booth or tool shed in service rooms. Each is a block you walk round, with one doorway facing the room's middle, and is furnished inside as its own little place (toilets and sinks, a bar counter, shelves). The room keeps its own furniture, cover and props round it. Pools, lagoons, hangar flight decks and airlock halls keep theirs along the walls so the middle stays open; food halls, rec rooms, fitness halls, container yards, maintenance bays and incinerator halls scatter them over the floor.
    - **Raised and sunken floor**: some rooms are built on more than one level, a metre apart. A **Terraced Walk** promenade has raised walks along its walls above a middle lane (or, when its doors open onto the walks, a court sunk below them); a **Sunken Court** atrium is sunk round its centrepiece; a **Split Deck** cargo bay is split into a raised deck and a lower floor; a **Seating Deck** food court has its tables up on a raised deck down the middle, with the food kiosks on the floor either side; a **Service Pit** hangar has a maintenance pit sunk into its floor, crossed by a gangway level with the floor, tool carts down in the pit and workbenches and tool racks round it. Flights of stairs join the levels where the routes from the doorways cross and every few metres along each edge; railings run along the rest. You always come in on the level of the doorway, every part of the room can be walked to and back, and nothing stands on the stairs or blocks their ends. Where an edge has no railing, you (and enemies) can drop off it, but you need the stairs to get back up. Outdoors the ground rises and falls too: a **Rolling Lawn** park has grassy mounds you walk up on any side, **Sinkhole Fields** a crater sloping down into the field, and a sunken court can have a bridge across it, level with the walks either side, with stairs down into each half.
    - **Most rooms have a second level**: nearly every room of reasonable size has some floor a metre up or down. Usually it is the same room with half of it, or a corner, standing a metre higher — its walls, pillars and furniture rising with it, stairs along the step (railed more often than not); otherwise a gallery or platform along a wall, or a sunken pit. Observation decks, bridges, the cafeteria, the promenade arcade, the spa terrace and the sculpture court have a raised or sunken area suited to them. Very small rooms (restrooms, junctions, cramped closets) mostly stay flat, and a feature is left out where it would crowd out a room's main furniture
    - **Floor in tiers**: any of these can go more than one level up or down, a level at a time, wherever the build allows that many levels and the room's ceiling leaves headroom (2 m over the highest floor). A stage can climb in steps, each step smaller than the one below and reached by its own stairs; a pit can deepen like an amphitheatre; a mound becomes a terraced hill with a ring of ground at each level, and a crater a pit ringed by ledges. Terraced rooms scale the same way: raised walks can step up twice to each wall (2-1-0-1-2), a sunken court can drop two ledges deep, a split deck can climb in two steps. Each place's layout says how many tiers it may have, and a room too small, too narrow or too low for them gets fewer, down to the single level it always had. No place uses more than one tier yet: it is switched on per layout. By default a room's floor keeps within one level of its doorways (one up, one down). Overpasses can cross over any lane at least two levels below them, so a tiered room can have its footbridge higher up. Decks like these aren't held to the floor's one-level range, only to the room's ceiling, so a tall hall can carry them higher than its floor ever goes
    - **Half walls, railings and open ledges**: a raised or sunken edge is either open (a one-metre step you can mantle up and drop off; enemies drop off it but take the stairs up), railed (a see-through rail you can't cross, see or shoot through) or walled by a solid **half wall** (waist-high from above, so you can crouch behind it and shoot over, and two metres from below, so it can't be mantled; it hides a standing person below it and counts as cover on both sides). Each area is built for a purpose: a **split level** (most raised halves and corners, platforms) is open or railed with stairs wherever a path crosses it; a **perch** (mezzanines, the arcade gallery, and about one raised half or corner in four) has a half wall along the side facing into the room and its other sides open to climb, reached by one flight when small and two when not — a raised half is walled all along, so its stairs are the only ways up; a **pit** (sunken floors) gets two ways out on different sides where it fits, and a pit with room for only one keeps its rim open, so none is a dead end. Where an area has more than one flight, the second climbs from another side or the far end, so there is always a flank rather than two stairs side by side
    - **Overpasses**: a Terraced Walk promenade can have a footbridge across its middle lane, from one raised walk to the other with stairs up at each end, and a Sunken Court atrium one across the court from walk to walk. Enemies walk underneath; the player has to crouch (1.8 m clear). Looking down from a walk, the deck hides whoever is under it. Enemies use it: guards that like high ground or overwatch may be posted up on the deck, and patrols walk up one flight, across and down the other as part of their round. The deck's stairs have rails along both sides.
    - **Height matters for sight**: standing, you see over counters, crates, low walls and the edge of a platform; crouched behind them you're hidden, and crouched on a sunken floor its rim hides you. Up on a platform you see over furniture that hides people on the floor below. Rooms are planned the same way: cover goes where someone crouched would have nothing to hide behind, a ledge counts as cover for the floor below it, and enemies looking for a firing position also take the edges of platforms and the rims of sunken courts.
  - **Placeholder columns**: columns are simple coloured shapes — round white columns in rings, square grey ones in grids and corners, green growth columns for BIO, blue pylons with a light band for TECH — stretched to each room's height
  - **Every place is furnished**: each place puts down the piece that says what it is first — a bar's counter, a theatre's stage, a bridge's command table, an engine room's turbines, a morgue's drawer wall, a hangar's shuttle — against a wall with room to walk up to it, leaving the middle of the room free for the room's chest, boss or terminal. Its furniture follows, more of it in bigger rooms: shelves lining a library's rows, seat rows in theatres and pews in chapels, bunks and lockers in barracks and cabin rows, consoles in control rooms, grow racks in hydroponics, crop beds on farms, machines in gyms and plant rooms, candles round a shrine. The pieces are simple coloured shapes until there is art: anchors carry an orange accent, furniture is brown and grey, machines yellow
  - **Places in many arrangements**: no place on the ship is one fixed room any more. Every generated place (all but the bare Junction and the hand-built Reactor Core) comes in two to four layouts, and every room of that place picks one, so the same place turns up in different floor plans, sizes and arrangements while always reading as itself. A few of them:
    - **Theatre** — an **Auditorium** of seat rows facing the stage, a **Cabaret** of card tables turned toward it with a bar, or a stepped **Amphitheatre** of benches. **Bar** — a **Lounge Bar** with booths, a **Pub** of tables, or a round **Cocktail Club** with sofas and a little stage. **Restaurant** — a **Dining Room**, a small **Bistro** or a long **Banquet Hall** of tables end to end, always with its kitchen line behind the divider
    - **Park** — a **Garden** of trees, hedges and benches, a dense **Grove**, or a **Plaza Garden** round a fountain. **Grand Atrium** — a **Fountain Court** or a **Galleria** of shopfronts. **School** — a **Classroom** of desks facing the teacher, a **Lecture Hall** of seat rows, or a **Study Room**
    - **Crew spaces** too: a **Bunk Rows** or **Bunk Bays** barracks, a cargo bay built round its dock doors (a **Container Yard** of containers in bays, a **Loading Dock** with pallets staged at every door, a **Dock Neck** from the dock through to pallet racks at the back, a **Split Deck**, a **Ro-Ro Deck** of vehicles parked in lanes, or a cramped **Logistics Module** with bags strapped in front of its racks), a storeroom racked the way real stores are (selective **Shelf Aisles**, block-stacked **Crate Stacks**, an **Aisle Comb**, a **Cage Store** of small lock-up units off a corridor, or a **High Bay** of tall pallet racks with a forklift), a **Turbine Hall** or **Drive Gallery** engine room, a **Command Deck** whose consoles face the command table or a **Flight Bridge** of console rows, **Server Aisles** or a round **Core Vault** data centre, and so on for every place
    - **Casino** — a long **Slot Hall** of slot banks back to back, with a bar and, in the biggest, a lounge stage and roulette; **Table Pits**, card tables in neat ranks round a ring of columns with rugs marking the pits and roulette in the larger rooms; or a small **High-Roller Salon** with a table or two, a bar and sofas. Every casino has its cashier cage on a wall and slot machines along its walls
    - **Library** — **Stacks** of tall shelving with study carrels and a reading nook; a grand two-room **Reading Hall** lined with shelves, reading tables in ranks down the middle among lamps and rugs; or a round **Rotunda** with shelves round the dome and sofas and tables in the ring. Every library has its front desk by the way in, with the card catalogue beside it
    - **Pool** — a long **Lap Pool** down the hall with jacuzzis at its end; a **Lagoon**, a big free pool (and a children's pool in the largest) with a water slide into it, jacuzzis and palms; or a small **Spa Terrace** with a plunge pool and jacuzzis. It also notes concealment it can see from a distance — each bush as the whole patch of ground it covers (where it lies, how big it is, which way), noted once it sees any part of it, not as a point in the middle; a bush it has never seen it doesn't know of, and kin pass on the ones they know well. Every visit makes a place more familiar. Places where it was hurt or saw an ally fall feel dangerous for a while. Kin standing close together tell each other what they know well, so a patroller who walked the far side tells the guard at the door. When an enemy's nerve breaks, it runs to the best place it knows for its kind, away from you: BIO to a bush it knows, TECH to a vantage, VOID into a corridor. If it knows none, it falls back as before. A guard posted on a vantage it has learned watches down that lane. An enemy that wandered in from elsewhere doesn't know the room yet, and has to learn it like anyone else. The spectator's *Paths & plans* overlay shows what the inspected agent knows
  - **Enemies find their own way round**: an enemy with no patrol route no longer stands at its post: it explores, going from one place of its own choosing to the next, round the ground near where it started (and inside its faction's territory). It remembers where it has walked and when, and is drawn to ground it has not seen lately and, less curious factions especially, to places it knows are good for something its faction values: Tech to firing positions, Void to hiding places. Somewhere it was hurt or saw an ally fall puts it off for a while, and it picks again at once. Kin close by tell each other what they have covered so a squad spreads out instead of combing the same corner. With a patrol route it still walks it, but now and then steps off at a waypoint to somewhere its experience points to before carrying on. Each faction sets how far it ranges, how curious it is, how often it strays from a route, or switches exploring off so its enemies guard their posts as before. The spectator's Plans overlay shows the ground the inspected agent has walked as faint cells that fade as they go stale
  - **Faction exploring style**: each faction explores in its own way. **TECH** learns its ground a little at a time: it starts close round its post, takes short hops to unseen ground next to what it already knows, and ranges further only as the ground it has covered grows, stopping to scan about every other stop. **VOID** works the outskirts: the edges of its range and along the walls. **BIO** keeps to the open middle of its ground and checks round the bushes and other concealment it knows. Squad followers explore on their own near their leader as well, and only close up when the leader gets more than 15 m away.
  - **Faction walking style**: how an enemy walks to a patrol stop or back to its post follows its faction. Bio slips from bush to bush on the way (where its type allows the detour); Void keeps to the walls in legs of a few metres, each pulled to the nearest wall or obstacle edge; Tech walks straight there. Where no wall is near the way, a wall-walker simply goes straight. Each faction's doctrine sets it
  - **Things that draw idle enemies**: points of interest in a level (grass to graze, a recharging station) attract the factions that like them. From time to time, when an enemy would choose where to go next, it may instead head for one in reach, walking there the way its faction walks, take a free spot, and stay a while facing it, taking what it gives (a recharging station restores shield, grazing a little health). Several can use a big one at once, the same enemy does not come back for a while, enemies keep away from where something bad happened and from other territories, and a fight or anything urgent makes them leave at once. A point can react to its users (grass shrinking, a station lighting). Nothing is placed in a scene yet: three kinds are ready, grazing grass for Bio and prey, drinking water for prey and Bio, and a recharging station for Tech
  - **Faction priorities**: each faction's fight rooms aim at their own mix of three traits, rated low, medium or high — **concealment** (floor to hide in: foliage and concealment patches), **walls** (narrow floor hemmed in by walls, pillars and furniture: corridors, aisles, corners that break sight) and **lines of fire** (long open lanes with hard cover beside them to shoot from; a long lane with nothing beside it counts a level lower — it's a killing field, not a firing line; low inner walls such as seat rows and counters are looked over, so they don't break a lane). A build setting (*Isovist For*) can instead have walls and lines of fire, the chokes and overwatch posts enemies take, and where cover goes read from what can be seen in every direction, so a diagonal lane or a round room's open middle counts; each is its own switch and all start off. **BIO** wants high concealment, medium walls, low lines of fire; **VOID** high walls, medium concealment, low lines of fire; **TECH** high lines of fire, medium walls, low concealment. The report shows what the holding faction wants next to what the room has, with the measurement behind each (share of floor concealed, share of floor at most 6 m across, longest lane and whether cover stands beside it), and the checks flag a trait one level off (note) or two levels off (warning) with what to change. A room no faction holds is only checked for a long open lane with nothing beside it. Generation follows the priorities in a faction's fight rooms: concealment patches come in half as many where concealment is low and half as many again where it is high (a room type's own concealment, like a Stealth room's, is left as it is), and each room's structure and then its furnishing are tried from a few seeds, keeping the try closest to the priorities, and a furnishing that still misses has its loose cover (the room type's cover and clutter, the faction's own pieces) moved about, one piece at a time, for as long as that brings the room closer — a room that already fits stays exactly as it was. The Room Lab says which furnishing try was kept and how many props were moved to fit. The rules behind room generation, a one-line card for each of the 48 places and the plan for the rest are in `ROOM_GENERATION.md`
  - Below the plan, the next eight seeds of the same room are drawn small, with any problems counted, so the spread a function produces — and the odd bad roll — shows at a glance; clicking one uses that seed
  - The room rebuilds as you edit: change a room function, dressing, shape or the build settings in the Inspector and the plan follows. *Play from here* plays a map room in MapGenerator
- **Blueprint furniture**: the Map Graph window's Blueprint has a *Props* toggle that draws every room's furniture and cover where the level places it
- One **Preview with** build settings (in the side panel's Graph section, and beside the Blueprint toggle) drives every preview at once: the rooms and enemy counts on the cards, the objective plan and the blueprint. Typing a new seed refreshes all of them, the blueprint included
- Locked nodes survive regeneration: the new map keeps a room of the same type at a similar depth on the same kind of route (main path or branch), with the same intensity
- The side panel checks the graph as you edit: exactly one Start and Exit, a Boss, the Exit reachable, every room reachable, every room-type rule (counts as the run's modifiers change them, placement, depth, neighbours, spacing, connection limit) still met, the pacing kept, every guaranteed room still in place, and every locked door still openable — it plays the map through, collecting keys, switching on terminals and opening doors, and flags any door whose key or terminal is missing, behind the door itself or behind another locked door (and a keycard lock given more than one key room)

## Seeds
- Everything generated has its own deterministic seed, branching from a parent: a map's layout and room types, each loot roll, each weapon. The same seed always produces the same result on every machine
- A layer's seed depends only on its parent and its name, never on what else was generated first, so changing how one system rolls can't shift another
- Seeds can be typed as numbers or any text ("banana" is a valid seed) — for maps and levels, weapons (`/weapon`, random weapon pickups) and vendors. Text is case-sensitive, and text that mixes letters and digits ("123abc") counts as text, not as the number 123
- Things generated without a chosen seed still get a random one and remember it, so any weapon can be reproduced afterwards
- Maps and weapons can reroll one layer at a time (see Map Graph Generation and Procedural Weapon Generation): a reroll only changes that layer, and a new seed starts every layer fresh

## Game Time
- A central game clock counts time in fixed ticks — 60 per game-second by default (one tick = 1/60 s) — independent of frame rate, for systems that need deterministic timing
- Pausing, slow motion and other time scaling all go through the clock, and Unity's physics, animation and movement follow it
- Several things can pause at once (pause menu, cutscene): time resumes only when all of them let go
- Speed changes stack: slow motion during a hit-stop is slower still; a speed change can last a fixed real-time duration (e.g. a 0.1 s hit-stop) and ends by itself
- Physics steps shrink with slow motion so it stays smooth
- Callbacks can be scheduled a number of game seconds or ticks ahead
- A long frame hitch runs at most a few ticks instead of freezing to catch up
- Quest time limits and temporary buffs run on game time, so they pause with the game and slow down in slow motion

## State Machines
- One reusable state machine for anything with states — the director's pacing, quests, bosses, doors, machines (enemies think with behavior trees instead)
- States switch when a condition becomes true, when a state or event asks, or forcibly (respawn/reset)
- "From any state" transitions cover interrupts like Stunned or Dead in one line instead of in every state
- A rule can forbid moves outright (nothing leaves Dead, a finished quest can't fail)
- Tracks the previous state and time spent in the current one
- Small machines can be built from plain callbacks, without a class per state

## Stats & Modifiers
- A generic modifier system for any stat: flat bonuses, percentage bonuses that add together (+10% and +10% = +20%), multipliers that compound (+10% and +10% = +21%), and overrides that fix a value outright
- Modifiers remember where they came from, so removing an attachment, a buff or a difficulty setting removes exactly its bonuses
- A character's damage, health and armor combine: base value → equipment (weapon attachments) → permanent presets (e.g. difficulty) → temporary buffs and debuffs
- Temporary modifiers expire on game time and are cleared when the character dies and revives
- Presets group modifiers into one asset: included are Hard Difficulty (enemies +50% health, +25% damage) and Damage Boost (+30% damage)
- Enemies with the Hard Difficulty preset get tougher without touching their enemy data

## Quests
- Quests are assets made of objectives: kill a number of a given enemy type, collect a number of an item, or wait for a scene event (talking to someone, reaching a place, pressing a switch)
- Objectives can be optional, or unlock one at a time in order
- Quests move through Locked → Available → Active → Completed or Failed; impossible moves (failing a finished quest) are refused
- Chains: a quest unlocks when all its prerequisite quests are complete, and can start automatically
- Time limits in game seconds; a failed quest can be allowed to retry
- Rewards (items or freshly rolled gear) go straight into the inventory on completion
- A quest panel on the HUD lists active quests with their objectives, progress counts, finished objectives struck through and a countdown for timed quests
- Firing range: the shooting range dummies stand in for faction enemies with their health, shield and armour; a destroyed dummy disappears and stands back up at full health after a few seconds. Each dummy's whole body is a solid block of its faction's colour (TECH orange, BIO green, VOID purple), so the faction reads at a glance even down the lane, and its sphere head is marked red as the headshot spot on top of a capsule body. The range is fully enclosed (roof included) and its wall and floor textures keep a fixed size however long the wall is. The firing line's low barrier can be jumped or mantled to get onto the lane, and an open doorway in the booth's east wall leads to a side room with a single dummy for testing melee and other close-range play. The switches change that dummy too. Six colour-coded switches on the firing line change the setup: faction (mixed, then TECH, BIO, VOID), tier (1–3) and how the dummies move (standing still, swaying side to side, advancing toward you and back, or strafing — quicker side-to-side runs that turn at random moments like a player dodging, for tracking practice) change every dummy, and distance pulls the lane dummies closer (25–100 m, 12.5–50 m or 5–20 m). The fifth stands every dummy back up at full health with no status effects, even mid-respawn. The sixth switches infinite ammo on and off: reloads take nothing from your reserve, so it never runs out (the ammo counter shows ∞), while magazines still empty and need reloading. It starts off. Distances are written on the lane floor every 25 m, and four low walls in the lane give cover to shoot over or vault. The round minimap (top-right) shows the range from above with fog of war that clears as you move. Each dummy blinks white when hit, even when its shield soaks the hit, and keeps a health bar over its head with a shield strip and the current health and shield numbers, so you can watch shield and health strip away. A panel on the left edge of the screen reports your hits on the dummies: target and distance, the last hit (headshots marked), hit and headshot count, damage, damage per second, and once the dummy drops, how long and how many hits it took. It starts a new fight after a kill or a few seconds without shooting, and the reset switch clears it. The prompt on each switch shows its current value
- Included: a two-quest chain for the shooting range — Target Practice (drop one firing-range dummy of each tier-1 enemy type — Maintenance Bot, Scavenger, Husk — pays 60 rounds) unlocks Resupply (pick up 30 rounds)

## Camera Effects
- Screen shake from "trauma": hits and explosions add trauma, which fades over time; many small hits blend into one shake instead of fighting each other
- Explosions shake every nearby camera, fading with distance
- Taking damage shakes the camera in proportion to the hit
- Each shot gives the view a springy kick on top of the weapon's aim recoil
- FOV punches that ease back (for dashes, boosts, explosions)
- Optional camera lag that makes the view trail sudden movement slightly (off by default — it's uncomfortable in first person)
- Camera transitions: blend the view smoothly to another viewpoint (death cam, door, boss intro) and back
- Effects are visual only — shake never moves where your shots go
- Lock-on (T): locks the aim onto the enemy nearest the centre of view and keeps it there; press again to release. The lock drops when the target dies or gets too far away

## Equipment
- Press Tab for the Character window: worn armor per slot (helm, torso, gloves, legs, boots, backslot), carried weapons, and what's in the pack
- Click armor in the pack to wear it (whatever was in that slot goes back to the pack); click worn armor to take it off
- Worn armor adds everything it rolled — armor, health, resistances — to the player, including the effect of any attachments fitted to it
- **Offhand**: every loadout slot has an offhand next to its main weapon (see Weapon Loadout). Shields, one-handed melee weapons and artifacts go there. It only counts while that slot's main is one-handed — pistols, SMGs, daggers, swords and axes — or empty. Under a two-handed main it stays in the slot, shown as unused (grayed out), and comes back when the slot gets a one-handed main. Shields found on a run go straight into the first free offhand
- **Artifacts** (relics, tomes, mirrors, lanterns — the Void's offhand items) also go in the offhand slot. They do two things, together or alone:
  - **Passive**: while held they give their rolled stats to you like worn armor — and a curse is just a negative stat (the Bone Idol sharpens your crits but drains your maximum health). Their perks work too: passive ones always, triggered ones (like armor's) answer to everything while it's held
  - **Active**: use it on the **right mouse**. An active artifact takes the right mouse, so a gun can't aim while it's held, the same trade shields make (and a melee weapon's own guard gives way to it). A passive-only artifact leaves the right mouse alone, so you keep your sights. V stays the weapon bash either way, so you can always parry
  - Three ways to use one: **tap** casts a spell, **hold** keeps something up while draining stamina (a ward), **hold** keeps a channel running (a buff). Hold uses drop when the stamina runs out or you can't act (dodging, stunned); a cast in progress is lost the same way
  - Artifacts roll a tier like other gear: better ones land better stats and are more **potent** (spells hit harder, channels give more). Cooldowns and charges only recharge while the artifact is held
  - The held artifact shows in a slot left of the ability bar: its name, whether right mouse taps or holds, how close it is to ready (or the stamina left while a hold is up), and its charges
  - **Hand Mirror**: hold to raise it; hits from in front are negated and thrown back at whoever made them. Drains stamina quickly
  - **Whispering Tome**: right mouse rends the enemy under your crosshair (45 damage, pierces armor). Two charges, a short cast, recharging over time or faster as you hit, kill and parry. Also adds a little status strength
  - **Bone Idol**: no active use. More crit chance and crit damage, less maximum health
  - **Oil Lantern**: hold to light it; steadier hands (25% less spread) and status resistance while it burns, draining stamina slowly
  - Found as loot (rare), in the Void's reality; held artifacts count as carried on a run, and one you're handed goes straight into an empty offhand
- Attachments: pick one from the pack, then click the armor or weapon to fit it; click a fitted attachment to take it back off. Some attachments only fit certain armor slots
- Attachments on weapons change the weapon for real: magazine size, reload time, fire rate and damage all follow them (e.g. Extended Magazine: +25% magazine, slower reload)

## Consumables
- Four item slots (keys 5–8 by default) for consumables and throwables, shown bottom-right with how many of each are left; the row of a grenade in hand lights up
- **Channelled items** take their use time, shown as a filling bar, and start when the key is tapped; they're only spent once the use finishes. Taking damage interrupts items that need concentration (the Bandage), as do being stunned, mantling or rolling; tapping a channelled item again cancels
- **Instant items** go off the moment the key goes down, so they can be woven into combos (eat between hits, or in the same moment as an ability). Each one puts **every** consumable on a short shared cooldown (shown as a grey bar draining over the consumable rows). Channelled items set no cooldown, so an instant item can follow straight after one finishes; pressing an instant item while channelling doesn't cancel the channel
- Using a consumable mid-combo holds the melee combo open, like an ability
- Effects: healing, removing all status effects, a timed stat buff, and restoring a resource meter
- Included: Bandage (1.5 s, heals 35 and stops bleeding and other effects), Combat Stim (0.5 s, +30% damage for 15 s and 50 stamina) and Ration (instant, heals 20, 1.5 s shared cooldown)
- Choose what sits in each item slot by holding its key (item wheel) or from the Character window. Holding an instant item's key uses one before the wheel opens; the **Hold Instant Item for Wheel** setting (Accessibility tab, on by default) turns the wheel off for instant items so that never happens

## Crafting
- Crafting stations in the world open a crafting window listing their recipes, what each needs and how much of it you have
- Recipes you can afford can be crafted instantly; the ingredients are taken and the result goes into the pack (armor is rolled fresh)
- Included recipes: Bandage ×2 (2 Cloth), Combat Stim (4 Scrap Metal, 1 Cloth), Extended Magazine (12 Scrap Metal), Combat Vest (20 Scrap Metal, 6 Cloth)

## Dev Console
- Backquote (`) opens a console in development builds; commands start with a slash (`/help`), with history (Up/Down). Escape closes it
- Auto-suggest: a hint line under the input shows the command being typed and the options for the word you're on — weapon categories, items, enemies, tiers, quest names, seed layers, `on`/`off` and so on. Tab fills in a single match or the start the matches share; pressing it again steps through them. If nothing starts with what you typed, it looks for names containing it
- `/help` lists every command; `/help <command>` adds an example and the options for its first argument. A wrong command suggests the closest ones ("Did you mean /weapon?"), and wrong arguments print the usage with an example
- Every item and weapon in the game can be handed out — new ones show up in the console automatically
- `/list` shows exactly what you can type (`/list weapons` → `AssaultRifle`, `Sniper`, …). Names never need quotes: spaces are ignored, so `/weapon assaultrifle` works. A name that matches several entries lists them
- While the console is open every other keybind is muted, so typing a letter never triggers a hotkey like the inventory, map or weapon slots
- `/give <item> [count]`, `/weapon <category> [seed] [tier] [layer:variant…]` (same seed, tier and layers = same gun), `/spawn <enemy> [count]`
- `/reroll <layer>` rerolls one seed layer (`quality`, `stats`, `perks`, or the character layer: `weapon` for guns, `melee` for melee weapons) of the weapon in hand and keeps everything else; it prints the seed with its rerolled layers (e.g. `seed 123 stats:2`), which `/weapon` takes back to rebuild that exact gun. Attachments and the loaded magazine don't carry over
- `/god`, `/heal`, `/buff <preset> [seconds]`, `/timescale <x>`, `/revealmap`, `/cloak [seconds]`, `/dodge [type]` (switch dodge style to try them out)
- `/overview` hides or shows the room overview: room type outlines and overhead labels on every room of a generated level
- `/room` lists the level's rooms; `/room next`, `/room back`, `/room <id>` or `/room <name>` moves you into one (asking for a name again moves on to the next room of that name). In a generated level or the Room Gallery
- `/quest list`, `/quest start|complete <quest>`, `/quest step <quest> <n>` to jump straight to any objective
- Names can be partial ("/give scrap 20", "/weapon sniper 42 legendary")
- `/save` shows where the ship save is and whether it loaded; `/save write` writes it now, `/save wipe` empties the hold and kit and deletes the save for a fresh start

## Minimap & World Map
- A round minimap in the top-right corner shows the area around the player, turning with the view so the player arrow always points up (or fixed north-up, per setting)
- Press M for the full world map: the whole area, every marker and the player's position and heading; the game pauses and controls lock while it's open
- Fog of war: the map starts dark and is uncovered around the player as they explore; things in unexplored areas stay hidden
- Markers for anything placed in the world — enemies, pickups, objectives — in a chosen shape (circle, square, diamond, or an arrow that shows facing), colour and size; markers can be minimap-only or world-map-only, disappear when their enemy dies, and objective markers stay pinned to the minimap's edge when out of range
- The map picture can be a top-down snapshot of the level taken automatically when the scene starts, a hand-made image, or — for procedurally generated runs — the map graph drawn as a schematic of rooms and connections in each room type's colour
- Explored areas can be saved and restored

## Feedback & Notifications
- One place for how the game responds to events: messages, screen flashes, controller vibration, camera shake and sounds are bundled into reusable feedback presets, so each reaction is tuned in one asset
- Message feed at the top of the screen for pickups ("+30 Standard Rounds", "Picked up …"), kills, quest updates and warnings, coloured by kind (info, success, reward, warning, danger); repeated messages stack into "×3" instead of flooding the feed, and old messages fade out
- Hit markers around the crosshair confirm every hit you land: white for hits, orange for critical hits, a bigger red marker for kills — also for grenade and ability damage
- Kill confirmation: "Eliminated Husk" (the enemy's name; the firing-range dummies carry the tier-1 enemies' names)
- Controller vibration for dealing and taking damage — bigger hits rumble harder, overlapping hits build up, and vibration stops while paused or when the game loses focus; can be turned off
- A low-health warning (message, red pulse, rumble) the moment health drops below 30%, re-armed once you heal back above it
- Quest announcements: new quest, objective complete, quest complete (with a golden flash), quest failed

## Stealth
- Step into a bush or a smoke cloud, or use the Stealth ability, and you become hidden: a HIDDEN label appears under the crosshair
- Hidden players can only be noticed by enemies right next to them (about 2.5 m); an enemy standing inside the same bush or smoke sees you from further away (about 6 m)
- Crouching makes you harder to notice, sprinting easier
- Some bushes only hide you while crouching
- Smoke also blocks enemies' line of sight through it, so you can cross open ground behind a smoke grenade
- Shooting, swinging or using abilities gives your position away for a moment (REVEALED); silenced weapons don't
- The Stealth ability hides you anywhere for 6 seconds; attacking ends it early
- If an enemy spots you while you're trying to hide, a "Spotted!" warning and a quick rumble let you know
- Enemies can still hear noise and come to investigate even when they can't see you
- Enemies know when they are hidden: the stealth system's verdict (in a bush or smoke, or cloaked, and not given away by an attack or a noise) is available to every enemy's behavior tree (*Hidden* and *HiddenFor*). While hidden with a target to deal with, each faction waits its own way (at most once every 15 s; it ends the moment the enemy is given away): **Bio ambushes** (stays still, eyes on you, until you walk within 5 m — or turn your back or reload within 12 m — then arms its Ambush and springs); **Tech scans** (sweeps its gaze across where it last saw you; if it picked you out it tells its kin where you are); **Void haunts** (stares from the dark and makes noises out beyond you to turn your head, and siphons you the moment you look away within 8 m)
- Enemies hide the same way: an enemy standing in a bush (or cloaked) is only noticed by rivals within about 2.5 m, or about 6 m when they stand in the same bush. Attacking, throwing a grenade or using an ability gives it away for a moment (except abilities that are themselves about hiding)
- Smoke grenade: bursts into a smoke cloud that lasts 15 seconds and deals no damage; the cloud shows as a churning clump of grey smoke that billows out over half a second and thins away over its last few seconds (enemy concealment abilities — TECH's Smoke Screen, Smoke Cover and Smoke Mortar, Bio's Plague Cloud and Spore Burst — show as a single round smoke-grey sphere instead)

## Generated Levels
- A scene can build its whole level from a map graph when it loads: every room in the graph becomes a room, and every connection becomes a corridor between the two rooms
- Rooms come in different floor plans: rectangles, L, T and U shapes, crosses, rings around an enclosed courtyard, round domes, halls with alcoves along one wall, stepped halls and long narrow halls, and three with straight 45° walls and sharp corners: **octagons**, **diamonds** and **pointed halls** (a long hall whose ends come to a 45° point). Each room varies the proportions (arm widths, courtyard size) and may be turned or mirrored, so no two rooms of the same shape look identical
- Every room is a place on the colony ship — Lobby, Restaurant, Park, Casino, Quarters, Cargo Bay, Grand Atrium, Docking Bay, Storage and more, one catalogue across all twelve categories — chosen to fit the room's role: fights happen in cargo bays, quarters, parks and restaurants, shops are restaurants, casinos or lobbies, resupply rooms are supply depots with an ammo cache against the wall, the boss waits in the Grand Atrium and the way out is a docking bay
- Places match the ship section they're in: the same Combat room is more likely quarters in Habitation, a restaurant or park in Commerce and a cargo bay in Engineering
- Each kind of place has its own floor plans, size and height: towering parks and atriums, low-ceilinged quarters, compact restaurants and casinos
- Big places take up more of the ship when there's space around them: cargo bays and parks can stretch across two rooms' worth of space, and the Grand Atrium can be a vast four-room hall — never at the cost of a neighbouring room
- Landmark rooms: hand-built places such as the Reactor Core (a towering reactor ringed by control consoles) can appear in place of a generated room, with doorways only where their design allows. A landmark is turned by whichever quarter turn points its doorways at the rooms it connects to (a Reactor Core whose doors face every way is turned at random, so no two look the same), and its furniture, walls and doorways all turn with it; a landmark can be set never to turn
- Rooms are furnished to suit them: dining tables in restaurant halls with supply crates in the kitchen, beds along crew-quarter walls, slot machines lining the casino, crates stacked along cargo bay walls, planters in parks, lobbies and atriums. Furniture faces sensibly (backed against walls, lined up with the room) and never blocks doorways or walkways
- Furniture and props follow placement rules rather than dice: wall pieces stand with their back to a wall, wide pieces lie along it, crates are stacked in piles and gather in corners, things you use (slot machines, resource nodes, ammo caches) always keep clear floor in front of them, and everything is spread out evenly instead of clumping or standing in ruled rows. A room is never packed solid: clutter stops at about a third of its floor
- Props stay out of walls: where a chair, lamp, plant or turned crate would reach into one, it (with the furniture it belongs to) stands that much farther off it, so the chair behind a desk and the plant in a corner stay in the room
- **Crates and cardboard boxes come in three sizes** wherever a room has them: small ones to crouch behind (still stacked in piles), medium ones (head-high crates; knee-to-waist-high boxes) and large ones (full-height crates that hide anyone; waist-high boxes). Bigger ones stand on their own and are rarer
- **Trees, bushes, rocks and barrels come in three sizes** too: saplings, trees and broad tall trees (only where the walls are 5.5 m or higher); low, ordinary and head-high bushes; knee-high rocks, boulders and head-high ones; small kegs, drums and tall tanks. A spot chosen to give cover never gets a smaller rock or barrel than usual
- **So do shelves, pallet stacks, lockers and cabinets**: low shelves you see over, full shelves and 3 m shelves (only where the walls are 5 m or higher); half, ordinary and full pallet stacks; narrow, ordinary and wide lockers, storage cabinets and filing cabinets
- **And tables, desks, benches and sofas**: small and long tables, compact, ordinary and executive desks (searched at the same odds as the cabinet sizes), short and long benches, armchairs and long sofas. Chairs, stools, lamps and rugs set round them move with the size, and a long one only appears where those still have room
- **Cargo containers and machines too**: short, ordinary, long (three tiles, only where the floor is free) and double-stacked containers (walls 6 m or higher); half-height, ordinary and wide server racks; small, ordinary and large fuel tanks and generators
- Whatever is placed, no part of a room is ever sealed off: every free spot of floor can be walked to from every doorway, and a column, crate or barrier that would cut off a corner or block a doorway simply isn't placed
- **Fights have cover.** Combat-type rooms (Combat, Breach, Lockdown, Holdout, Rift, Hazard, Stealth and the Boss arena) get low barriers placed where they break the longest open lines of fire, turned to face the lane they block, set away from the walls so both sides can be used and with gaps between them to run through; crate piles fill the corners and a few crates line the walls. Bigger rooms get more
- **Cover reads at a glance** (the AI Simulator arena's rule): every broken column, wall stub and counter is either low cover — 0.9–1.2 m, grey, crouch behind it or shoot over it standing — or high cover, 2.2 m or more and white like the walls, that hides someone standing. Nothing is left chest-high, too tall to shoot over and too short to hide behind
- Treasure rooms, and the Ambush that poses as one, get the same crate piles and wall crates, so an Ambush still looks exactly like a Treasure room
- A Quiet room's resource node, ammo cache or loot cache is tucked into a nook against a wall, off the routes and doorways with floor in front of it. Cargo bays, docking bays and storerooms always get their ammo cache, a park always has a resource node
- Optional ceilings close every room and corridor at its own height; open-air places (parks, farms, observation decks, the town square) are not roofed but sit under a **simulated sky dome**: a vault rising from the top of the room's walls, pale at the horizon and deeper blue toward the zenith, as on the habitat decks of a colony ship. The dome meets the room's top edge all round, whatever its shape, and is only to be seen: it casts no shadow and cannot be walked on or shot
- Shapes suit their place in the map: hub rooms with many connections favour crosses and domes. Rooms are always one connected space, with every passage at least two tiles (6 m) wide
- Domes and rings have smooth, rounded walls (big ones, 24 tiles across or more, get one more rounding pass, so a large dome's arc is finer than a small one's): the stair-steps of a round floor plan become an arc of short wall pieces with gentle bends (about 11° each by default), never moving the walls beside a doorway
- Room corners are bevelled at 45° by default — the ship-interior look — one tile deep, two in the Grand Atrium and Docking Bay. Only outside corners are cut (never inside ones), never where a doorway is close by, and nothing is placed in the cut-off space; corner props move to the spots on either side of the bevel. Corridor bends are bevelled on their outside corner too. Both can be turned off, and each kind of room can set its own depth
- Walls wear a modular art kit instead of plain boxes: wall panels repeated along every wall, angled pieces on bevels and rounded walls, posts at corners and on either side of each doorway, and a frame around every doorway. Each reality has its own placeholder kit: plain colour-coded blocks with no decoration, so the reality reads from the colour alone:
  - **TECH** (the ship's own luxury interior, also used for corridors): light grey walls
  - **BIO**: olive-green walls
  - **VOID**: near-black walls
  A room wears the kit of the faction that holds it, so the reality is readable from the walls alone
- **Planned look (not in the game yet; see the GDD's Art Direction).** The placeholder kits stand in for three distinct styles, each with its own colours, cultural influence and weapons:
  - **TECH** — white and warm colours, organized and unified, form follows function, with a modern Japanese industrial feel: clean white panels with orange and red trim, labelled signage, tidy conduits. Its melee weapons are engineered blades, staffs and batons, and its guns are modern and energy weapons
  - **BIO** — earthy greens, overgrown nature and biomutation with a European medieval flavour: vegetation and fleshy growth through the walls, nests and carcasses, "hunt or be hunted". Its melee weapons are forged medieval arms and shields, and its ranged weapons are bows, crossbows and early black powder guns
  - **VOID** — gloomy blues and violets, American cult classic horror noir: smoke, cold, hard light and shadow, mirrors and shrines, hauntings and cursed artifacts. Its melee weapons are occult tools and everyday objects turned weapons, and its guns are revolvers, lever action rifles and double barrel shotguns
  - Places are strict, portable things are loose: rooms, large props and enemies belong to one faction, while items, gear and weapons can come from any reality and mix freely. Each weapon family gets a look for each reality, and weapons are not locked to a faction
  - Firearms are not Tech-only: each reality's guns come from a different era, and the melee weapons follow each reality's culture. Black powder guns share the same scarce bullets as every other firearm
- The Map Graph window's blueprint shows the bevels and the curved walls exactly as they're built
- **Each faction builds in its own way.** A room is built by the reality that holds it, so the three read differently before you see an enemy (a Breach room carries both realities' props):
  - **TECH — ordered, efficient, defensive.** Mostly rectangular halls and crosses, laid out on a regular grid of columns shared across the whole ship. Production lines of machines run nose to tail down the length of the room with an aisle between each line; server racks stand in banks along the walls in mirrored pairs, so the left of a room matches the right, with an operator's console and chair facing each bank; sentry posts flank every doorway, each behind its own steel barrier like a checkpoint. A low checkpoint wall crosses the middle of a room and funnels everyone through a gap on the walkway. Rooms are clean and uncluttered, and the cover in a fight is tidy steel barriers and containers
  - **BIO — open, overgrown, nature won.** Round domes, rings and wide open plans. The old structure is in ruins: columns missing or snapped off at different heights, stubs of collapsed wall standing alone. Trees grow through the floor with thickets of bushes gathered round their roots, an overgrown resting spot or two stands against a wall (a bench between bushes, sometimes with an old lamp post), overgrown patches (see-through thickets you walk into, which hide whoever stands in them) are spread evenly over every part of the room — about three to five for a mid-sized room, up to twice that in a big hall, and half as many again in fight rooms — so there is always somewhere to hide nearby, fallen logs and mossy crates lie around, and thick moss and grass, with rubble, cover the floor — even across the walkways, with glowing spores here and there. The floor itself is **overgrown**: a low drift of dirt and moss (up to about 0.3 m) creeps out from a few patches where the growth broke through and piles up against the walls, in soft winding lumps. It never slopes steeper than a gentle bank, so you and enemies walk across it as on bare floor, and it stays clear of doorways, stairs, ledges, furniture, the centre spot and the floor beside low cover, so it never makes a ramp onto a platform, buries cover or changes how the room plays. Rooms on the frontier of BIO territory are less overgrown; in a room BIO breaches, the growth keeps to BIO's side Cover is broken wall slabs, logs and overgrown crates
  - **VOID — noir, narrow, crowded.** Small bent rooms (L, U and T plans) built as 1930s underworld offices: narrow halls running the length of the room with rows of offices either side, full-height walls between them and a framed doorway into every office, so any corner could hide anything. They are packed with furniture, always in sets that belong together: office desks in a row along a wall, each with its chair behind it, its visitor's floor in front and now and then a desk lamp; runs of filing cabinets side by side; a sofa against a wall on a rug with a lamp beside it; an interrogation table with its chairs pushed back and a street lamp standing over it; a safe in a corner. Nothing is strewn about at random — and a fight's cover is a desk kicked on its side, wooden crates and filing cabinets
  - A column or wall a function already brings (a restaurant's kitchen wall, a casino's colonnade) is kept; a faction's own structure fills in where a room has none. Whatever a faction builds, every room stays walkable from every doorway
- Rooms have structure: rows of support columns in lobbies, cargo and docking bays (lined up across the whole ship), colonnades circling atriums and casinos, columns framing the inside corners of L, T and cross rooms, kitchen walls in restaurants, cabin walls in crew quarters and a bar counter in casinos
- **Buildings and towns**: a school, hospital or library is a building with the same interior wherever it stands. A hall runs in from the front door with rooms along both sides, each entered through its own doorway, and optionally one big room across the far end. Every room is furnished as its own place, so a school has a principal's office beside the entrance, a teachers' lounge, classrooms, restrooms, a supply closet, a library and a gymnasium at the end of the hall, a hospital a reception and waiting room, wards, operating rooms, a pharmacy, a lab, storage and a morgue, and a library a circulation desk, reading rooms, stacks, study rooms, an archive and a rotunda. A plan names the rooms, how many and how wide; each building gets a different arrangement every time, and rooms the building was too small for are left out rather than squeezed in
  - Fights in a building are close quarters: a hall one or two tiles wide, doorways one at a time, no sight lines past the room you are in
  - **In a ship room** the building is one of the place's arrangements (the School, Medical Bay and Library places each have a *School Floor*, *Hospital Floor* or *Library Floor* next to their single-room ones): a room in a corridor wall with a door, and the whole interior behind it. **In a town** the same interiors stand among streets, each walled in with one front door onto a street. Streets and plazas are the open counterpart: long sight lines and room to move, while every building is confined, so a fight can move from open ground to a doorway and into a room. Houses, a school, a hospital and a library stand among them; some lots are left as plazas, and the streets are furnished like a promenade
  - **A town is optional**: the **Town Square** is one more room a map may roll, at most one a map and not on every map. Nothing needs it: schools, hospitals and libraries come up on their own as ordinary rooms. A map can also pin the Town Square, or a building arrangement, on a node. The Room Lab lists a building's rooms under *Spaces* and shows each as its own zone
  - **Roofs and lights**: buildings in a town have roofs (the streets stay under the sky dome), even when the level builds no ceilings, and a soft light in every hall and room, since the roof shuts out the sky. A building in a ship room is closed in by its room's own ceiling, like any room
  - A room can take one of its place's arrangements: a school's classrooms have rows of desks facing the teacher, a hospital's operating rooms their tables and its wards their beds, the lounge its seating. A plan can pin an arrangement to a room or let each room pick its own
  - **Restroom** is a place of its own: toilets and sinks along the walls and a bin. Schools, hospitals and houses have them (a house's is its bathroom)
  - A room of a building gets the arrangement's furniture only, not its inner walls: a classroom's desks stand in the room as the building made it
- A clear walkway always runs from every doorway to the middle of the room: no column, wall or prop is ever placed on it, and inner walls always leave gaps where it passes, so no part of a room is ever walled off. Inner walls are only built when both sides are a room's worth of floor (at least 8 tiles), never slicing off a sliver
- Doorways sit on straight stretches of wall — at the end of an arm rather than tucked into a corner — never open into a ring's courtyard, and face the room they lead to
- Rooms sit where the graph places them, so the main path runs left to right and branches sit above and below it. Corridors are routed around rooms, and two different connections never join up. The rare map where two corridors have to cross is reported as a warning
- Locked and secret connections get their gate (a locked door, a breakable fake wall) on the side the player arrives from, so a locked branch is locked from the main path
- Generated levels have working gates out of the box: locked doors are yellow bulkheads that take a Security Keycard (one is placed for every locked door, always reachable before it, and used up on opening), secret passages are hidden behind a fake wall that looks like the others and breaks when shot, and one-way shortcuts are cyan doors. Ordinary passages stay open
- A one-way shortcut's door is barred from the near side ("Barred from the other side") and opens for a player coming from the deep end; after that it opens both ways
- Door signs: a glowing bar over every doorway, in the colour of the room it leads to (the same colours as the maps), so every fork is a readable choice — a Shop, a Treasure room, a Resupply stop. Doorways into a tier-3 room, the Boss, a Lockdown, Holdout or Rift, or any high-intensity room also show a red danger marker; an Ambush's sign shows Treasure. Secret passages get no sign. Under each sign the room beyond is named (MEDICAL BAY, CARGO BAY…), with the faction that holds it in its colour and its tier, so you know what you are walking into
- Room overview (for designers): in a generated level (Map Generator scene) and the Room Gallery, every room is outlined along the tops of its walls in its room type's map colour, and a label lies flat above its middle, readable from overhead in the Scene view: its number (as `/room` uses it) and place in capitals, then its real room type in that colour — an Ambush shows as Ambush, "signed as Treasure" — and its layout, then the faction holding it and its tier, ship side, how deep in its faction's territory it stands and who breaches it. `/overview` in the dev console hides or shows it all; the Level Builder's *Room Overview* box turns it off
- The first time you walk into a room the HUD feed names it, with its faction and tier
- Each locked door's key is placed in the room the map chose for it. Terminal-locked doors are orange bulkheads, with a terminal console (red light until switched on) in each room the map chose; the door unlocks once all of them are on
- Rooms are filled by type: enemies (more in higher-intensity rooms, patrolling out to the far ends of the room), a centrepiece (for example a chest in Treasure rooms — anything and everything, 2–3 draws — a crafting station in Shops, a boss) and cover and clutter that suit the room type, kept off columns, doorways and walkways
- The player starts in the Start room, a safe hub with a workbench. The Exit room has the extraction pad that ends the run in victory (hold Interact to extract)
- The MapGenerator scene plays a freshly generated level every time it starts or restarts, with the player in the Start room, a minimap, the world map (M) and HUD notifications. It runs the full expedition loop: the player starts with only the starting loadout (no dev ammo), death ends the run, and the ship screen between runs leads into the next one
- A level can use a fixed, hand-checked map graph or generate a fresh one each play. The same seed always gives the same map, the same rooms (kind, shape and structure) and the same room contents
- What a level rolls during play comes from its seed too, so a kept seed is the same run, not just the same map: what every chest, cache, crate, breakable, resource node and encounter or objective reward gives, what each enemy drops and which affixes tougher enemies roll (enemies arriving in encounter waves included), how a gamble machine's pulls come out, and what a shop sells. It doesn't matter in which order the player gets there — each room and each thing in it has its own seed. Only moment-to-moment combat (crits, recoil, status procs) stays random. A vendor or random weapon pickup with a seed of its own typed in keeps it
- The world map and minimap resize to fit the generated level and show its floor plan: room shapes coloured by room type, walls, bevels, curves and gate markers, uncovered by fog of war as the player explores
- A generated level announces the run's modifiers when it starts

## Character Animation
- The player and enemies drive their model's animations from what they're actually doing: walking and strafing speed, sprinting, crouching, sliding, mantling, rolling, jumping and falling, aiming, reloading, firing, melee combo steps, grenade throws, getting hit, being stunned and dying
- Enemies switch to an alert stance once they notice a target, and play their attack as the wind-up starts
- Animations are cosmetic: attacks land on the same timing with or without a rigged model, and characters without one play exactly as before
- Respawned players and reused enemies start from a clean pose instead of the end of their death animation
- Sounds can be tied to moments inside an animation (a footfall, a magazine leaving the gun) instead of a timer, so they stay in step with the clips; the player's footsteps can follow the walking animation this way and still match the surface underfoot. Characters without such animation keep the timed footsteps
- First-person weapon animation: every weapon shares one set of states (draw, fire, dry fire, reload, aim, sprint) and brings its own clips, and each weapon can have its own magazine and chamber sounds. The weapon is drawn again whenever it is swapped in or taken out of holster

## Impacts & Ragdolls
- Bullets, projectiles, enemy shots and melee hits leave per-surface impact effects: a decal (bullet hole), a burst of particles and an impact sound, each chosen by what was hit (concrete, metal, wood… by physics material). Characters use a separate "flesh" set
- Melee and bullets can look and sound different on the same surface
- Decals stick to moving objects such as doors, and disappear with objects that break. Only the newest ones are kept (128 by default), so long fights never pile up
- Characters with a ragdoll go limp when they die and are knocked away from whoever landed the killing blow. They get back up in a clean pose when revived or reused

## Economy & Shops
- Money is carried like any other item. Credits are the standard currency, and more currencies can exist side by side (each shop names the one it takes). Anything that can hand out items can hand out money: loot, pickups, quest rewards, the dev console (`/give credits 500`)
- Vendors sell from a shelf that is stocked when the level starts, and optionally restocked on a timer. The same vendor seed always gives the same shelf; a vendor without one stocks from the level's seed in a generated level, and at random elsewhere
- Goods can be bundles of stackable items (30 rounds, 2 bandages), rolled gear, or freshly generated weapons, each vendor mixing whatever its catalog lists
- Prices follow each item's worth: stackables per unit, rolled gear and weapons scaled by quality (a Legendary is worth several times a Common of the same kind). Vendors can charge a markup
- Items can be limited in number (sold out until the next restock) or unlimited
- A bought weapon goes straight into a free weapon slot. With every slot full it replaces the weapon in hand, which the vendor takes in trade and credits against the price. The shop shows this before you buy
- The player can sell items from their pack for a fraction of their worth. Cheap bulk goods like ammo sell in the smallest bundle worth a coin (Shift sells the whole stack). Nothing can be bought and sold back for a profit
- The shop window lists the shelf in tier colours with prices, what's out of reach (can't afford, no free slot) and the player's balance

## Dialogue & NPCs
- NPCs can be talked to with the Interact key. They turn to face the player and open a dialogue window with their name, their line and the player's replies
- Conversations branch: replies lead to other lines, loop back, or end the talk. The first four replies can also be picked with the number keys
- Replies can do things as well as move the talk along, such as opening the NPC's shop. Replies can also be shown only when a condition holds (for example, only while the player carries a certain item)
- Closing the window walks away from the conversation at any point
- In the ShootingRange scene, the **Gunsmith** greets the player. "Let's trade" opens a shop with ammo for every caliber and a rotating selection of randomly rolled weapons. The player starts with 1,500 credits
